Nielsen's last large systematic work, published by Schubothe in 1873, the same
year as the essay »Et Synspunkt for Darwinismen« in *For Ide og Virkelighed*.
The programme is in the subtitle: *Bidrag til en med Physiken stemmende
Naturphilosophie* — a contribution to a philosophy of nature that **agrees**
with physics. The word does the work. Nielsen is not proposing to legislate to
the physicists, and he says so at length before he begins; he is proposing that
there are questions the science of experience cannot take up without ceasing to
be itself, and that these are the questions a philosophy of nature exists to
take up.

The *Indledning* (pp. XI–XIV) sets the enemies on both sides. Against the
popular scientism of the 1870s: natural science, "naar den holder sig indenfor
Erfaringens Grændser," cannot own as its task the solving of the riddle of
existence, and the many mutually combating world-views that appeal to
"Videnskaben" cannot all have its support. That a famous investigator holds it
probable that man is a modified ape does not make the opinion a fact, still less
a dogma; a distinction is to be made "mellem Kjendsgjerningernes Udtydning og
Kjendsgjerningerne selv." Hence the book's best-known sentence about hypotheses:

> Enhver Hypothese har, saa at sige, to Ansigter, et, strengt, alvorligt, der er
> vendt imod Kjendsgjerningerne og ufravendt sigter paa Opdagelsen af deres
> skjulte Sammenhæng, et andet, smilende, mere indsmigrende, der tiltaler
> Almeenheden og vil erobre sig en Plads blandt dens Yndlingsdoctriner.

*Every hypothesis has, so to speak, two faces: one severe and earnest, turned
toward the facts and aiming unswervingly at the discovery of their hidden
connection; another smiling, more ingratiating, which addresses the general
public and would win itself a place among its favourite doctrines.* And against
the other side — Schelling, Hegel, "Immanentsphilosophien," which is the book's
standing adversary — the philosophy of nature "hverken skal bygge paa
Hypotheser eller paa abstracte Speculationer."

## The argument

Everything rests on a doubling stated in three sentences of the *Indledning*
(p. XIII). The collected impression of all contemplation of nature is an
impression of power: "Naturen er et Magtens Rige." Power shows itself from the
sensuous side as a visible power in the series of phenomena, each member
presupposing and pointing to another — otherness-power, outward power, *Magt i
Andet*; and from the non-sensuous side as the invisible power of thoughts, of
concepts, of ideas — *Selvmagt*. Out of this doubling the philosophy of nature
develops its two concepts: "af Magten i Andet Begrebet *Natur*, af Selvmagtens
Væsen Begrebet *Aand*." Both terms are letterspaced in the print. The method
follows at once, and is modest: "Erfaringslæren fremstiller Naturen i dens eget
Lys; Naturphilosophien fremstiller den i Aandens Lys. Vil den forandrede
Belysning være i Stand til at aabne os nye Indblik i de mechaniske, elementære
og organiske Natursphærer? Det maa komme an paa et Forsøg." *It must depend upon
an attempt.* The three spheres named in that question are the three Parts of the
book.

**Part One, Den mechaniske Natur (pp. 1–196).** Foundational concepts first
(space and time, matter and force, forces and laws), then mechanical causes,
then the mechanics of the heavens. The cause-chapters are the most technical
philosophy in the book: force resolved into accelerating, moving and living
force; cause resolved into condition, cause and conditioned cause; then the
question whether the moments of the cause are ideal or real, answered
"ideal-real." The astronomical chapter is written as a history with a thesis —
the Ptolemaic structure as the illusion, the Copernican as reflexion, and then
"the discussion: three hypotheses" — running through Kepler's laws and
gravitation to the universe as a system of systems, and closing on the origin of
the solar system. There Nielsen grants the nebular hypothesis its physics and
refuses its metaphysics: the primal nebula with which the beginning is made must
itself have an origin, so *Oprindelse* and *Begyndelse* are not the same
question, and the old dispute between teleology and mechanism is not settled by
pushing the mechanism further back.

**Part Two, Den elementære Natur (pp. 197–374).** The elements, their functions,
and the elemental whole. Nielsen opens historically — matter and element in the
Greeks, matter and form as the simplest reconciliation, matter and substance in
the atomists — and then gives his own account: the elements have an ideality and
a reality, and beneath both a *Grundmedium*, a ground-medium, which is where the
doubling of power reappears at this level. The functions are classified by what
they do: light and heat are **dynamic**, magnetism and electricity
**articulating** (*leddelende*), chemism **body-forming**. Each of the first two
is taken twice over, from the objective and the subjective side — "Uden Syn
intet Lys," without sight no light — and then as an ideal-real nature. The Part
ends on the body of the earth, treated as one elemental individual: atmosphere,
crust and sea as its comprehending parts; land, sea and air, the zones and
climates, the ocean and the great continents as its articulation; then the
geognostic, the aesthetic and the teleological standpoints; then its history in
three periods. Its last word is a paradox Nielsen wants: the earth is a
*selfless* organism, an *inorganic* organism — organised through and through and
yet with no self-being of its own.

**Part Three, Den organiske Natur (pp. 375–549).** Life is introduced as
"organiserende Selvvæsen," organising self-being, over against the being of
matter; the organism is not life's vehicle but life's product and property. From
this Nielsen derives the *fundamental law of recollection* (Erindringens
Grundlov), which he uses against Eduard von Hartmann's *Philosophie des
Unbewussten* at p. 400: a philosophy that must let a new soul begin life over
again in every revived organism has mistaken the originality of self-being. The
first chapter then works through individuals, species and genera to the
systematics of the basic forms, which is where Darwin enters (below). The second
chapter is the book's anthropology in ascending order: the plant is life without
soul, an organism without limbs; the animal has soul and body, sense-
consciousness, and a weak individuality; and then — "Mennesket er ikke et Dyr" —
man, through self-consciousness, conscience and personality. Nielsen concedes
comparative anatomy everything it asks (the unlikenesses between an ass's head
and a man's are "forsvindende"), and locates the difference where the anatomist
does not look.

The concluding chapter, *Slutning: Natur og Aand* (pp. 509–549), is the book in
miniature, in three theses: **nature is created by Spirit**, **nature is
sustained by Spirit**, **nature is explained by Spirit**. The first asks whether
nature *can* be created and answers by examining three relations — of essence
between self-power and power in another, of ground between absolute freedom and
conditioned necessity, and of cause between Spirit's will and nature's
constitution. The second turns on the unalterability of the laws of nature,
which Nielsen will not soften: sustaining is not intervening. The third is a
theodicy of a sort — discord and harmony, enjoyment and suffering, life and
death — and it is the hardest stretch in the book. Death releases the individual
but is the enemy of life's idea; if life's dying is to last for ever, "saa er
Modsigelsen ikke paa Dødens, men paa Livets Side," and since nature's highest
would then be a contradiction, and nature comes from Spirit, Spirit would be the
original contradiction. Nielsen answers with the two fundamental laws —
recollection on the side of Spirit, real development on the side of nature — and
with the claim that the laws of life require of nature itself a second,
ideal-empirical actuality that we do not know, and therefore a "Naturforvandling,"
a transformation of nature. The last sentence, letterspaced in the print:

> Naturlivets Maal er et Aandens Maal, Magten i Andet er Selvmagtens reale
> Moment: al Naturvirkelighed er i evig Forstand *Aandsvirkelighed, Virkelighed
> for Aanden.*

*The goal of natural life is a goal of Spirit, the power in another is
self-power's real moment: all actuality of nature is in the eternal sense
actuality of Spirit, actuality for Spirit.*

## The text

Complete, and complete in both languages. The Danish transcription covers the
whole book: *Indhold* (pp. V–X), *Indledning* (pp. XI–XIV), body pp. 1–549, and
the unfoliated *Trykfeil* leaf — 549 of 549 pages, no gaps and no duplicates.
The English translation covers the *Indledning* and the whole body, pp. 1–549,
and is made from the transcription rather than from the scan. Division and
chapter heads are given in English with the Danish kept in a comment above each,
so the two files stay checkable against one another line by line. The *Indhold*
is not translated — the English contents are generated from the heads — and
neither is the *Trykfeil* leaf, whose entries correct Danish type.

## Editorial note

**Two physical copies.** A Google scan serves as the working text and the Royal
Library's colour scan (DOD) as the second witness, and both were needed. The
Google file is 1-bit JBIG2, a symbol-substitution codec that can swap one glyph
image for another silently, so no doubtful single letter was settled on it;
worse, the copy Google scanned had been worked through in pencil by a previous
owner applying Nielsen's own errata, so at an errata site the working scan is
liable to show the corrected reading rather than the printed one. All 32 errata
sites were therefore read on the KB copy. The Google file is also a Sammelband:
its last 118 leaves are the 1870 *Grundideernes Logik i kort Begreb*,
transcribed separately. The "689 pp." this catalogue once gave for the book was
that PDF's page count.

**Page maps**, both uniform and both verified whole: printed = working PDF − 21,
checked against that file's text layer for all 689 of its pages; printed = KB
PDF − 19, checked by content match on 23 samples from p. 2 to p. 549, since that
file has no text layer.

**Three folios are misprinted in the type** and agree in both copies: 357 prints
as "257", 394 as "294", 395 as "295". The pages are marked here by their true
number and the misprint logged at the site.

**Diplomatic throughout, in the English as in the Danish.** The book's own
corrections are not applied; each is noted at its site so a reader sees both
what was printed and what the author wanted. Turned and wrong sorts stand as
printed — *Betydniug*, *Huusholduing*, *Reprodnctionen*, *Erindriugslov*,
*Mnlighed*, *maattc*, *hunne* — as do trebled letters (*gjennnem*,
*Fosterdannnelsen*), missing stops, and the division head on p. 233 that prints
"B." where the sequence and the errata leaf both require "C." The most striking
defect is not a letter at all: **the first chapter of the Third Part breaks off
in mid-sentence at the foot of p. 444**, "…maaler sig med den Darwinske," with
no closing word and no full stop, followed by the closing rule and the next
chapter. It is so in both copies and the errata leaf is silent about it. It is
reproduced as printed in both files; nothing has been supplied.

**Mathematics.** Unlike every other book in this collection, this one contains
real mathematics. Of the 549 printed pages, 83 carry mathematical setting —
counted by taking every page in `transcription.tex` with math mode in the body,
excluding the errata leaf — most densely through the mechanics of Part One
(pp. 64–115) and the elemental functions of Part Two (pp. 251–286), though some
of that is chemical formulae and temperatures rather than algebra. The high
point is the footnote to p. 160, which runs over two leaves and derives all
three Keplerian laws from Newton's principles in polar coordinates, integrating
to the conic-section orbit, the area law, and $T^{2}/a^{3} = 4\pi^{2}/\mu$.
Nielsen's notation is kept as he sets it, including his own minus sign ÷ and his
*tang* and *Radius vector*; the formulas were copied from the image, not
retyped from OCR, which is not a witness on those pages.

The comment header of `transcription.tex` carries the same record at greater
length, page by page, and remains the authority: it was written at the image
while the transcription was being made.

## Reading

**The Darwin chapter (pp. 424–444) is the reason to read the book now.** It is
not the anti-Darwinian tract the date and the author might suggest. Nielsen sets
out the hypothesis at length and largely in Darwin's own words — species and
varieties, the laws of variation, the fertility of hybrids, with long quotations
from the *Origin* cited by page — and grants it its empirical case. His
objection is to the systematics, not to the descent: a classification founded on
the physiological alone is "abstractly teleological," and he proposes instead a
"continued system of naturally conditioned creations," in which acts of creation
set the qualitative boundaries and physical descent supplies the transitions
between them. The conclusion is more accommodating than any of his readers seem
to expect. So far is the doctrine of peculiar, nature-grounding acts of
creation from standing in conflict with the Darwinian hypothesis, he writes, that
it "tvertimod, hvad Principet angaaer, har i denne Hypothese sin bedste empiriske
Støtte" — on the contrary has, as regards the principle, its best empirical
support in that hypothesis. A footnote on p. 444 goes further: "Mennesket kan —
i sit Anlæg — være skabt Millioner Aar, før det traadte frem paa Jorden," man
may, in his constitution, have been created millions of years before he stepped
forth on the earth. Then the sentence breaks off.

**The relation to the rest of Nielsen.** The book cites *Grundideernes Logik i
kort Begreb* at pp. 118 and 443, on the rational and the antirational, and the
doubling of power into *Magt i Andet* and *Selvmagt* is worth setting beside the
*uensartede Størrelser* of the 1849 controversy: whether the later doctrine is
the earlier one carried into the philosophy of nature, or a different
distinction wearing similar clothes, is a question this edition makes it
possible to ask but does not settle.

**What is not here.** The philosophy of nature, Nielsen says at p. XIV, "betragter
jo ikke Naturen for Aandens Skyld, men omvendt, Aanden for Naturens Skyld" — it
considers Spirit for the sake of nature, and does not set out to exhaust the
doctrine of Spirit. Readers coming to the book for Nielsen's theology will find
it standing just off the edge of every page, and nowhere argued.
