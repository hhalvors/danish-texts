*Om det Religiøse i dets Enhed med det Humane. Et positivt Supplement til
„Problemet om Tro og Viden“* (Copenhagen: P. G. Philipsens Forlag, 1869; [IV] +
211 pp., printed by Bianco Lunos Bogtrykkeri) is the book Hans Brøchner promised
at the end of the 1868 volume. *Problemet om Tro og Viden* had been
historical-critical, and its result, as Brøchner says on his first page, "from
the nature of the case, had essentially to be negative": faith in the sense of
positive religion and knowledge are incongruent, and cannot be held together in
one consciousness without discord. This is the positive half — the attempt to
show that everything the positive religions place on revelation can be carried
by a rational ethics, and that the religious and the human, so far from being
opposed, are one. Both books are in this collection, in Danish and in English,
and they are meant to be read as a pair.

## The text

Complete, in both languages: printed pp. 1–211, comprising the Indhold, the
untitled Indledning (pp. 1–4), divisions I (5–29), II (30–63) and III (64–201),
the Slutning (202–210), and the author's *Trykfeil* leaf (211). Transcribed
from the Royal Danish Library scan of the copy at shelfmark DA 1.-2.S 3 8°.

The three divisions are:

- **I. Om Enhedsforholdet mellem den sande Erkjendelse og den sande Handlen** —
  that true cognition and true action stand in a relation of unity conditioned
  by the unity of essence.
- **II. Om Tilstedeværelsen af det i sand Betydning Religiøses væsentlige
  Kjendemærker i den sande Erkjendelse og den sande Handlen** — that the marks
  of the religious, faith and self-surrender, are already present in them.
- **III. De centrale ethisk-religiøse Problemer, opfattede fra Videns
  Synspunkt** — the freedom of the will, the essence and possibility of evil,
  and reconciliation, worked out from the standpoint of knowledge. It is by far
  the longest, and it carries the book's two extended critical sections, on
  Feuerbach (pp. 97–126) and on Hegel (126 ff.).

## Editorial note

This is a diplomatic edition. Nineteenth-century orthography stands as printed —
*Christendommen*, *Theologie*, *høiere*, *aa*, capitalised nouns — and printer's
errors are transcribed as printed, logged in a comment at the site, and never
silently corrected. The quote-balance counts therefore drift off zero on
purpose.

**Page map.** Uniform across the whole book: printed p. N = PDF page N + 11, so
printed 1–210 are PDF 12–221 and the *Trykfeil* leaf is PDF 222. Verified by
rendering the head band of every leaf and reading all 211 folios by eye; the
offset holds with no exceptions. Three leaves carry no folio by design — p. 1,
p. 5, and the errata leaf.

**Emphasis.** The book's only emphatic device inside a paragraph is
letterspacing (*Sperrsatz*), and it is used heavily, on whole phrases rather
than single words. Bold Fraktur occurs only in display heads and in the
top-level entries of the Indhold, never inside a paragraph. All letterspacing is
rendered `\emph{}`.

The compositor is *inconsistent*, and this is the edition's most persistent
technical problem. A run often stops at a line break, or inside a word, or
inside a word that is not broken at all: `Aands`-*functioner* (p. 6),
*Tids*`bestemmelsen` (p. 18), `Væsen`*stotaliteten* (p. 19), *Livsforhold i
Be*`tydning` (p. 32), *Væsens*`bestemmelsen` (p. 178, whole word, spaced in the
first element only), *ideelle Mo*`ment` (p. 190). Twice a single run is carried
across a page break inside a broken word — *Syn-* / *dens Begreb* at pp.
194/195, and *Syn-* / *den selv* at pp. 195/196. All of it is reproduced rather
than regularised. Where the English compound divides at the same joint it
divides there; where it cannot, the run falls on the corresponding element and a
comment records what the printing does. Either way the **run count** is
preserved, which is what the parity check tests.

**Antiqua and Greek.** Latin words are set in antiqua within the Fraktur, and
the antiqua may itself be letterspaced or not, page by page — *causa sui* and
*causa finalis* are spaced on p. 68, *modus* (p. 83) and *eo ipso* (p. 89) are
tight, and *conditio sine qua non* on p. 59 is half and half. The clearest case
is p. 192, where *sub specie æterni* stands twice on the one page, spaced at its
first occurrence and tight nine lines later. Spaced antiqua is encoded
`\emph{\textit{…}}` and tight antiqua `\textit{…}`; the distinction is invisible
in English, where italic already does duty for emphasis, and is kept
mechanically so the two files stay comparable. Proper names stay in Fraktur and
are letterspaced instead — not one name was found set in antiqua anywhere in the
book. Greek is always antiqua italic with accents; neither OCR witness reads any
of it, so all 53 runs were caught by eye, page by page.

**Rules.** Thirty-four short centred rules stand in pp. 1–210, and the book uses
them in two roles which this edition treats differently. Sixteen are head
ornaments, standing immediately above or below a display or run-in head; they
belong to the setting of the head and are not transcribed. The other eighteen —
division-closing rules and mid-page thought-breaks — are transcribed uniformly.
Five of them are printed as *double* rules, at pp. 4, 29, 63, 201 and 210, that
is at the close of the Indledning and of each division and of the Slutning;
every other rule in the book is a single hairline. The distinction is recorded
at each site rather than reproduced typographically.

**Footnotes** are printed `*)` and `**)` and reset on every *printed* page —
proved on pp. 16 and 17, which each carry two. `footmisc[perpage]` is wrong
here, since it resets on the typeset page.

**Where the Indhold disagrees with the heads.** In eight places the book's own
contents leaf and its printed heads genuinely differ, and both readings are
recorded rather than normalised into agreement — the disagreement is a datum.
They range from a single letter (p. 90, head *Det menneskelige Væsens Begreb*,
Indhold *De*) through enumerator styles (`1)` for `1.` at pp. 190 and 192,
`2)` for `2.` at p. 164) to word order (p. 168, head *α) Ophævelsen af Skylden*,
Indhold *α) Skyldens Ophævelse*). The Indhold also contradicts itself: it places
*2. Forsoningen, dens Kilde og Maal* at 167–201, though the head is printed on
p. 164 and its own first sub-entry is given as 165–167. Two body heads have no
Indhold entry at all (pp. 193 and 195). Six rubric lines appear in the contents
and nowhere in the body, and are not introduced into it.

For this reason the Indhold is translated as a list rather than replaced by a
generated table of contents, which is what the sibling volume does. Here it is a
bibliographic object in its own right, and all of it is reproduced.

**The author's errata.** The *Trykfeil* leaf carries three blocks, and only the
first belongs to this book: p. 62, line 24, printed *fra det*, to read *for
det*. It is transcribed as printed at p. 62, flagged there, and **not applied**.
The other two blocks are errata for *Problemet om Tro og Viden* (nine items) and
for *Et Svar til Prof. R. Nielsen*, 2det Oplag (two items), and are reproduced
as printed. In the English the leaf's rubric is translated — *Side* → Page,
*læs:* → read: — while the words being corrected stay in Danish, since each
erratum is about a Danish sort in a Danish line and has no referent in the
English. Note the heading's spelling: *Trykfeil:*, with *-ei-*.

**Twenty-two further printer's defects** are logged at the site: *hinandes* for
*hinandens* (p. 7), *Inhold* for *Indhold* (p. 26), *Tilintetgjørrelse* with a
doubled r (p. 43), *Asolutes* for *Absolutes* (p. 68), a doubled article in *i
den den gode Villies* (p. 167), *man* for *men* (p. 168), *Naturexisteniserne*
(p. 169), *gjennm* for *gjennem* (p. 195), and a comma standing inside *Dogmet
om det, Supranaturale* (p. 202), among others. Most are orthographic and do not
survive translation; the two that do — the doubled article and the stray comma —
are reproduced in the English. Several claimed defects were examined and
rejected, and are recorded as rejected so they are not re-litigated.

**How the translation was checked.** Not by reading, which catches almost none
of this, but by counting both files mechanically with comments stripped. The
final figures, over pp. 1–211:

| | Danish | English |
|---|---|---|
| emphasis runs | 2086 | 2086 |
| footnotes | 41 | 41 |
| footnote-counter resets | 36 | 36 |
| transcribed rules | 20 | 20 |
| antiqua Latin | 36 | 36 (+1 on the title page) |
| display and chapter heads | 21 | 21 |
| run-in heads | 8 | 8 |

The one extra antiqua run is the Danish original title, set in italic beneath
the English one on the translation's title page: an editorial addition, not a
divergence in the body.

The check earned its keep. It found a title italicised in English that the
printing sets plain (p. 1); a dropped run on *bevidst* (p. 21); three places
where one Danish run had become two English ones, or lost a half; a quotation
pair supplied at p. 32 where the printing marks the quoted phrase with a colon
alone; a printed dash pair flattened at p. 59; two wrong sentence-final marks
(pp. 106 and 164); and, most seriously, two passages at pp. 120 and 122 where a
quotation had been rendered as reported speech, silently dropping the printing's
quotation marks. None of these was visible on reading.

Punctuation *within* the sentence is not under that rule. The translation sets
more em-dashes than the Danish, as every other completed pair in this collection
does — the sibling volume at a ratio of 1.3, others considerably higher — since
rendering a Danish comma-bounded apposition with an English dash pair is
ordinary translation practice and not a defect. What the dash count is good for
is the opposite direction: a page where the English has *fewer* dashes than the
printing may have dropped one the compositor actually set. After the p. 59
repair, no page in the book does.

## Reading

The argument of division III is the part most likely to interest a reader coming
from Kierkegaard or from the Danish Hegelians. Brøchner's problem is that a
rational ethics has to account for guilt without borrowing the machinery of the
positive religions: without a will whose law is heterogeneous with reason,
without an inherited corruption of nature, without a satisfaction accepted by
divine fiat. His solution turns on a claim about the structure of the evil will
— that in willing itself in isolation it works with a force borrowed from the
essence it is fighting, hollows itself out, and generates in the essence a
discord that shows up in consciousness as anxiety, and that at the point where
that anxiety becomes anxiety for the essence's own dissolution the relation
reverses and repentance becomes possible (pp. 152–164). Reconciliation is then
not a transaction but a redintegration, and the force actualised by the evil
willing is *converted*, not annulled: repentance, "in opposition to what Fichte
would have it," effects an increase of force (p. 172).

Three conditions have to be met for the ideal to be actualised (p. 168): that
guilt can be made good, that the ethical can become a determination of totality
for a human life, and that the individual life can acquire a determinateness by
eternity. The third is where the book is least comfortable. Brøchner rejects
personal immortality in the ordinary sense — an existence continued into
infinity would give only "an infinitely continued approximation," never
eternity's correlate (p. 183) — and what he offers instead is the individual
spirit taken up "as a moment in the Eternal's life," with a *moment of
completion* at death in which the divided force is gathered into an undivided
energy, for every individual whatever the kind of the will has been (pp.
186–189). Whether that preserves anything a reader would recognise as the person
is a question the book raises and does not settle.

The Feuerbach section (pp. 97–126) is the fullest engagement in Danish
philosophy of the period, and it is generous: Brøchner takes the projection
thesis seriously as an account of what the positive religions do, and then
argues that it does not touch the religious relation as he has defined it.
Feuerbach's man makes himself poor in order to enrich God; Brøchner's does not
have to, because the principle is immanent. The closing comparison of divisions
III.1 and III.2 (pp. 190–201) sets his own view against Spinoza and Hegel on one
side — both, he argues, share an abstract conception of eternity that dissolves
the individual and with it the ethical — and against the Christian doctrine of
reconciliation on the other, where the argument becomes sharp: election of grace
is the point at which the arbitrariness of the divine will "comes forward in its
greatest sharpness," and Calvin's *et horribile decretum* and Luther's gloss on
the Gospel are quoted to show that the consistent theologians saw it.

The Slutning is worth reading on its own. It is partly a summary, partly a
programme — "the religion of actuality, which is *not* to be founded or created,
but only to be shown as being" (p. 206) — and partly a settling of accounts. The
last three pages dismiss a "so-called *Religionsphilosophie*" (Rasmus Nielsen's)
for postulating rather than proving the self-limitation of knowledge, issue an
open challenge to Danish theologians to attack his criticism directly rather
than indirectly, and answer in advance any charge of atheism "only with the
silence which is that of contempt."
