%%  The University: Rasmus Nielsen and Sibbern  [Ch. II paragraphs 2, 3, 4, 5]

\section*{The University: Rasmus Nielsen and Sibbern}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The University: Rasmus Nielsen and Sibbern}
\label{sec:universitetet}

When as a young student I walked up the university stairs, it was not the first
time I had gone to lectures at the University. During my last years at school I
attended, for several semesters, Carsten Hauch's lectures on aesthetics. One
semester he lectured on the folk ballads, another on Ewald's \textit{Adam og
Eva}, a third on a tragedy of Shakespeare's. His investigations did not go very
deep, but when he became enthusiastic he could say excellent things, and his
whole figure was then as though transfigured, the noble upper part of his face
carrying off the victory over the somewhat drooping lower part. I preserve a
vivid recollection of the mood of those lectures. --- In my last year at school
I also attended the lectures of Fog, later a bishop, on the study of theology.
They did not prove very orienting for me as a theologian in the making. I
remember only one image, meant to make vivid the coldness of rigorous science
as against the warmth of faith: Athena is a lofty and noble goddess, but she
did not spring from any mother's womb and has suckled no mother's breast. That
image pursued me for a long while, until I discovered that it did not fit;
for rigorous science is itself an expression of the human spirit, conceived and
nourished by it. It has its mother in a human need which is bound up with what
is innermost in personality. But it cost a great deal of labour before I could
see the matter in this way. For the time being, philosophy and theology stood
as two poles between which I felt myself stretched. Neither of them could I let
go; but how were they to subsist together! Must there not, in the end, be some
single expression here for the life of personality? ---

It was Rasmus Nielsen and Sibbern who became my first teachers in philosophy.
Rasmus Nielsen stood at that time at his height. He was still searching, taken
with Kierkegaard and absorbed in mathematical and scientific studies, striving
to find a path by which these different worlds of thought might be united. His
propaedeutic lectures consisted, in my year, partly of a going-through of
Kierkegaard's \textit{Stadier} [Stages on Life's Way], on the basis of a small
selection he had had printed, and partly of a short outline of the theory of
knowledge. He spoke with life and force, often with wit and spirit, and I owe
these lectures an extraordinary amount. They set a mass of thoughts stirring in
me which until then had not moved at all. Yet it went with that course as it so
often went with his books: the design was too large and comprehensive, or else
he worked out the first parts of it at such length that there was not time
enough left for the later ones. Of the \textit{Stadier} he thus managed to go
through only the first, the ``aesthetic'' stage. At every possible opportunity
he polemicised against theology as a science, drawing a sharp distinction
between faith and theology. This polemic at first provoked my contradiction. I
had come with great expectations of what the study of theology was to bring me;
I hoped that the isolation in which the religious had hitherto stood within my
inner life would, with theology's help, give way to a harmony between the
different spheres. I hoped to be led into a union of the life of thought and
the life of feeling, such that they supplemented one another; I hoped that
Athena and the Madonna might be made one. In a diary I kept during my first
years as a student I accordingly complained bitterly of those who would not
acknowledge that there is such a thing as a Christian science. --- It was soon
to be otherwise.

While Rasmus Nielsen lectured on philosophical propaedeutics, Sibbern lectured
on psychology and logic. (The philosophical course laid claim in those days to
ten hours a week. That was perhaps too much. But people have gone too far in
restricting it to four hours, even though a good deal can now be accomplished
in that time by the use of printed texts. In my opinion six hours would be
right.) The reason propaedeutics was separated from psychology and logic was
that it was meant above all to give the theory of knowledge, or a general
doctrine of science. Rasmus Nielsen, for that matter, gave a great deal besides
the theory of knowledge under the heading of propaedeutics --- in my year, for
instance, the survey of the Kierkegaardian stages. There was a third professor,
Hans Brøchner; he doubled alternately for Sibbern and for Nielsen, and in my
year he lectured on Sibbern's subjects. I had chosen Sibbern, since I wished to
hear him before he retired, and since I expected much of the author of
\textit{Gabrielis}. He was then seventy-seven. It proved a disappointment. There
came, to be sure, occasional flashes of genius, fine thoughts and precious
remarks; but his delivery was flat and mechanical, and his dulled senses let
him neither hear how he himself rattled his keys while he spoke, nor notice the
restlessness and chatter of his listeners. His naivety and oddity could be
delicious --- as when he named his own \textit{Gabrielis} among ``good poetic
works'', or when he explained to us at length why the four clocks of the Church
of Our Lady did not keep the same time. But when, because he was too warm at
the lectern, he took off first his skullcap and then, when that did not help,
his wig, one could not demand that young people should take it with reverence.
He had outlived himself as a teacher, though his inner life still stirred with
freshness. His books were heavy going to learn from, the more so as he
preferred to have his own words given back to him. --- Only much later, when I
had myself become independent in philosophy, did I learn properly to appreciate
him, and it has been a great joy to me to bring forward his memory and to
characterise him,\footnote{See \textit{Mindre Arbejder}, First Series, and
\textit{Danske Filosofer}.} and to help make his thoughts known abroad as well.

In my first year as a student I also began my theological studies, especially
exegesis and church history. Exegetical study is probably what has been of most
use to me within theology. It accustomed one to analysis of what one read, to
precise and searching understanding, and it gave occasion for interesting
comparisons among the New Testament writers. And to become at home in so great
a work of the spirit as the New Testament, to be able to range through it in
every direction and to feel the connection of the thoughts with the linguistic
form, was already a great gain; and it is above all this that has made me never
regret having studied theology, however far I have travelled from theological
ways of thinking. To be sure, the diffuse German commentaries we studied were
no pleasure to get sense out of. One ploughed through a whole series of
interpretations which in the author's eyes were mistaken, and at last arrived
at the author's own reading, which was of course the highest wisdom. There was a
pedantry in this which could give one a distaste for the study. And when one
reflected properly that the words which were the object of all this learning
were meant to place their readers before life's greatest question, the question
of spiritual life or spiritual death, one could often be indignant at the whole
manner of treatment --- especially just after reading a book like Kierkegaard's
\textit{Til Selvprøvelse} [For Self-Examination]. Several times I have flung the
commentaries across the floor --- but afterwards I went and picked them up
again. I did, after all, want to finish what I had begun. ---

%% ============================================================
%%  Kierkegaard and the Dark Years  [Ch. II paragraphs 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 17]
%% ============================================================
\section*{Kierkegaard and the Dark Years}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Kierkegaard and the Dark Years}
\label{sec:kierkegaard}

There now came a heavy and dark time in my inner life as well. I immersed
myself in Kierkegaard's writings, and after a hard struggle I had to grant him
that he was right in his conception of Christianity. Christianity no longer
stood as the mild, consoling way of truth which could take up into itself all
human ways of living --- family life, art, science, the life of society --- and
lead them towards their completion. The idyll was blown apart. The ideal of the
world beyond came to stand in its whole unconditional severity. Only one thing
was, in the literal sense of the word, needful: to enter into relation with the
great paradox, the wonder of the becoming-man of God, which made everything
human turn pale and set only one task, that of giving up everything in order to
have a share in the divine powers which, at a single place in the world and at
a single point in time, had broken through the connectedness of nature and of
human life and let the light of eternity shine into the darkness. Sayings in
the New Testament which I had hitherto pushed aside, or interpreted mildly and
harmonisingly, now came to stand in their whole and absolute ideality. It was
not Christianity's meaning to complete what human culture had begun, but to
break it off and to leave all human relations standing as matters of
indifference, in so far as they were not hindrances to reaching the great goal.
It fell from my eyes like scales when I now took up the New Testament afresh.
And it stood before me that what was proclaimed there was the highest. I cannot
point to any definite moment of conversion. It was after hard resistance that I
gave up the harmonious and idyllic faith of my early youth. That Christianity
was, and would be, consolation, the eternal consolation, was true; but this
consolation was tied to one condition: full surrender to the one thing needful,
full renunciation of life's human ends. It was really only now that I
discovered that neither art nor science nor civic activity is mentioned in the
New Testament, and that marriage is warned against in severe terms. It was
Christian ethics that now showed itself to me in a wholly new light. With
dogmatics there was for the time being nothing the matter.

How was life now to be led, once the ideal had shown itself to lie higher than
I had thought? The time that now came brought me, it is true, moments of
heartfelt enthusiasm, but above all it brought me doubt, unrest and
homelessness. I did not know what to do. The master, the prophet, had departed,
after saying that the Christianity of the New Testament did not exist --- if
indeed it had existed at all beyond the very earliest time. It was in vain to
speculate about how he would have positioned himself had he not been carried
off so early. And besides, he had himself taught me that each single individual
must solve the problem of life by his own way --- that fundamentally one cannot
learn from others.

And now my theological studies! I worked with zeal at the various theological
subjects, but continued my philosophical studies as well, attending Rasmus
Nielsen's evening lectures regularly, and now Brøchner's on the history of
philosophy too. I took part in a \textit{privatissimum} which Brøchner held on
Kant's \textit{Kritik der Urtheilskraft}. But from the purely philosophical side
it was maintained ever more firmly that a scientific harmony between faith and
knowledge was impossible --- that fundamentally there was no such thing as a
theological science. So I wandered back and forth between theology and
philosophy --- literally as well, since their lecture rooms lay at either end of
the long university corridor upstairs. A constant intellectual discord asserted
itself in me, over and above the personal one called forth by the new
conception of Christianity.

Those were the heaviest and darkest years of my life. I shut myself up with my
doubts and became unsociable and closed towards those nearest me. However much
I cared for them, I was not a good member of the family. Life in my room and
life in the sitting room came to stand before me as two worlds, and I was only
too poor at making the transition between them. The bright and harmonious way
in which they took everything down there exasperated me. It was just in those
years that increased prosperity came into the house, and people enjoyed, in
their fashion, what it now gave access to. I held myself aloof and cold. I have
many sins on my conscience from those years, and only later have I understood
how greatly I had to be borne with. My parents were, as I have since learned,
anxious about me, and asked my friends for advice.

Through all this there also worked an inclination to hypochondria, which is
bound up with my bodily constitution and has followed me all my life, right up
into old age. Only those closest to me have had occasion to notice this
tendency, which as a rule I find it easy to force back in the presence of
strangers. In my book \textit{Den store Humor} [The Great Humour], the section
dealing with hypochondria (\textit{acedia}) is one of those most built upon
self-experience. Heaviness and darkness could spread over the mind without
demonstrable cause and give everything a dull and foggy appearance. For a long
time there often came over me a sensation as though I were hanging by a hair
which might snap at any moment. Dissatisfied with myself and with the fact that
I was accomplishing nothing in the world, I often felt that everything was
hopeless for me. This tendency naturally gained the upper hand all the more
easily in that my mind stood doubting and restless before the greatest
questions. What the balance of force was between the two currents --- the one
coming from bodily heaviness, the other from the weight of intellectual and
personal problems --- I naturally cannot disentangle, and could do so still less
at the time.

But it was not, of course, that I went about perpetually as one lost. My
studies occupied me greatly. Besides my professional subject, the interest in
Hebrew already awakened at school led me to read Arabic with Professor van
Mehren. I got so far as to read a couple of tales from the \textit{Thousand and
One Nights} in the original, and for a while I even thought of becoming an
orientalist --- which would probably have worked to draw me off from my
absorption in philosophical and religious problems. I still read a fair amount
of philosophy, and followed in particular the development from Kant to Hegel, a
period which at that time especially stood in a peculiar splendour. Not only had
Kierkegaard been strongly influenced by Hegel, but both Rasmus Nielsen and
Brøchner represented shades of Hegelianism. I also read some dialogues of
Plato's in the original. In short, my time was well filled.

\begin{center}[\,\ldots\,]\end{center}

Besides Valby there was another place to which I often walked out, and where I
found rest and understanding. An old uncle of mine lived at a country house in
Frederiksberg Smallegade, and his house was kept by a distant relation of my
parents, Sofie Pape, sister of the young girl I had visited towards the end of
my Jutland journey.\footnote{The two sisters were daughters of the landowner
H.\ C.\ Pape of Heiselt in Vendsyssel. For a time he also held a tenancy near
Viborg. (See above, p.~36.)} In her I found the elder sister I had often wished
for. She understood my interest in Kierkegaard, though she kept in the main to
his religious discourses. And now, in the winter of 1863--64, her younger sister
Emma came to visit her. We three came to form a little clique. But it was soon
for the younger one's sake that I went there. We read together and we walked
together. And I supposed now that I had acquired a sister of my own age. That it
was a feeling of another kind that was stirring in me became clear to me only
after her departure. For the time being I forced back everything that stirred in
that direction, under the influence of the severe religious discipline I had put
myself under. And yet, far more than I would admit to myself, the thought of her
was in those dark years a gleam of light that kept courage and hope alive. There
lay here, besides, the germ of something that later worked to bring me into a
living relation to life again.

%%  The Reckoning with Kierkegaard  [Ch. II paragraphs 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]

\section*{The Reckoning with Kierkegaard}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The Reckoning with Kierkegaard}
\label{sec:opgoret}

I had not given up Christianity, but I made to myself the admission which
Kierkegaard (in \textit{Indøvelse i Kristendom} [Practice in Christianity]) had
demanded of the representatives of the Church. I acknowledged that I was not
living under the ethics which primitive Christianity presupposes or entails. I
recognised my distance from the primitive Christian ideal, though I held fast
that it was the ideal. I felt like a Catholic who does not enter a monastery,
although he admits that this is the ideal. I held that on these terms I had the
right to regard myself as a Christian, even though I could not think of becoming
a clergyman.

The Church as Church could not --- as I have since understood --- make the
admission Kierkegaard demanded. It would have amounted to admitting that it
represented a wholly different religion from the primitive Christian one, once
a religion and its ethics are acknowledged to belong together so closely that
they stand and fall with one another. But the individual, too, who makes the
admission is really conceding --- and this I did not see clearly at the time
either --- that he does not have the religion the Church claimed to represent.
It signified in reality a break with the Church, in virtue of one's own
experience of life and one's own thinking, and I thereby placed myself under the
principle of personality. The reach of this dawned on me only slowly and late,
in connection with my continued experience of life and my continued studies. On
this point Kierkegaard himself, in virtue of the principle that subjectivity is
truth, points far out beyond himself. My love for Kierkegaard and my admiration
for him have therefore been able to accompany me long after I had moved away
from all dogmas. It has, to be sure, been said of me in German, on the occasion
of my book on Kierkegaard, by some light-footed theologian, that I lack love for
Kierkegaard and absorption in him. But the fact is that there are people who do
not understand a love and an admiration which do not vent themselves in
sentimental expressions, but in taking up a problem that has been posed and
working at it faithfully in the way that accords with one's own personality.
Kierkegaard's problem has followed me from my youth, has determined the
direction of my life, has again and again led me to a more inward conception of
manifold relations, to a stricter testing of myself, to a shyness of whatever
lacks personal truth. And while theologians and half-theologians, in the
centenary year of his birth, were whitewashing the prophet's tomb, I held to the
great principle of subjectivity as truth and sought to apply it in the way that
accorded with my personality and my studies.

Purely philosophically, the study of Kierkegaard led me to a thought which my
later philosophical study went on to deepen and to apply in detail: namely, that
the formal peculiarity of spiritual life lies in the unity, the concentration,
with which the content of life is gathered together, and that the measure of the
worth of a spiritual life lies in the relation between the fullness of the
content and the energy of the concentration. This thought, which has appeared in
the history of philosophy before Kierkegaard, underlies Kierkegaard's
philosophical characterisation of the different stages or types of life. This
point, naturally, was not clear to me then either. It dawned on me only through
a critical study of English philosophy and through the preliminary work for my
\textit{Psykologi}.

There was another point of view from which, in those years, I was likewise not
yet able to judge Kierkegaard's conception of primitive Christianity. Theologians
are accustomed to regard the thought which carries the whole primitive Christian
ethics --- namely the expectation of the imminent arrival of ``the Kingdom of
Heaven'' --- as an inessential illusion of the earliest congregation, or else
they explain away the passages where it is spoken of with particular force. And
to my great astonishment I saw, when Kierkegaard's posthumous papers were
printed, that he himself regarded the expectation as an illusion --- only that
he inverted the relation: in his view one could not live by an ethics such as the
primitive Christian one without that illusion. ``It is to such a degree agonising
to be the true Christian that it would not be endurable if one did not
constantly expect Christ's return precisely now.'' (\textit{Efterladte Papirer},
1849, p.~248.) By calling the expectation an illusion, Kierkegaard sets himself
in complete opposition to primitive Christianity. From a historical standpoint
the expectation is a fact, and is the constant positive presupposition without
which the ethics of the New Testament cannot be understood. I saw this clearly
when later on I began my reading of the New Testament over again. Yet I had
already earlier had an inkling of this connection, in that already in my student
years I felt sympathy for Irvingism, which attempted to build precisely on the
actual expectation (``precisely now''). In the history of religion I have since
learned to see the place of the New Testament expectation within a whole series
of apocalypses or prophecies within the Jewish world. --- But I return to where I
stood in the autumn of 1864.

Besides the reason given above for my not wanting to be a clergyman, there was
also another reason, which now, as I look back, strikes me as fully as essential.
I could not conceive of having to step forward at a fixed day and hour (Sunday at
ten o'clock!) and speak about what was the most inward and the highest of all
things to me. The moments in which anything of that kind stirred in me came as
gifts I could not reckon on in advance or call forth at will. Nor was I master of
my hypochondriac states, in which what had stood clear and true in the good times
was suddenly darkened and seemed to be nothing. To this was added a shyness ---
I might as well call it a chastity --- which made, and makes, me unwilling to
speak of what moves me most inwardly. I have also since experienced all too often
that what moved me most strongly in a quiet hour of preparation had no effect
when I came out with it from a rostrum.

\section*{From Theology to Philosophy}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{From Theology to Philosophy}
\label{sec:overgangen}

Among my theological teachers it is Hermansen to whom I owe most, especially so
far as this year is concerned. It had long since become clear to me that his
hyper-orthodox interpretation of the Old Testament was untenable, and that his
attempts to repel what he called ``the encroachments of the newer criticism''
had failed. But I continued to take an interest in reading the Old Testament in
the original. I taught Hebrew myself and had, as already mentioned, also read a
little Arabic. And as regards my last year of study, I owe Hermansen much for
his learned and thorough going-through of the papers we wrote at the exercises
he held. It was the theology of the seventeenth century that furnished the
presuppositions of his expositions on the subjects we treated; but for that very
reason there was a firmness and a consistency in the train of thought which
modern theologians of Clausen's and Martensen's tendency did not offer. And then
it did one good to work something out oneself, to be a little active after so
much receptivity. I still think with a certain pleasure of those Saturday
afternoons when I sat in Lecture Room No.~6 and worked out my exercise papers.
Hermansen himself was a solid Jutlander, naive and dryly humorous, friendly and
helpful.

\begin{center}[\,\ldots\,]\end{center}

They were hard problems that I took with me from the period of my life which had
now closed. I had preserved my personal faith, with the reservation I described
earlier. But in what relation was this faith now to stand --- and how could it
stand --- to whatever else occupied me in life? That was the chief question that
followed me. And to answer it, philosophical thinking was necessary. Philosophy,
which had hitherto been a secondary subject for me, now became a principal one.
For the answering of that question demanded an investigation of what human
knowledge is and what it can do, and in what relation it stands to personal
life. I had to learn what the human life of the mind is able to reach on its own,
in order then to be able to see how far a connection between it and religious
faith was possible, and on what terms. That was the problem the Kierkegaard
studies had confronted me with, but had not solved. I had to pursue this question
further. My future I pictured to myself provisionally in this way: that teaching
should be my outward occupation, with such studies attached to it as it demanded
--- but that I would then strive to have a closet of my own where what lay
personally on my mind could be cultivated. That my philosophising might come to
have any significance whatever for others did not occur to me.

And so, after the summer holidays, I began my work as a teacher. Ever since my
schooldays I had been interested in historical reading, and I now planned a study
of the most important historical works, from Herodotus and Thucydides to Macaulay
and Ranke. I also thought for a moment of competing for a prize essay the
University had announced on a subject from the Danish Middle Ages; but nothing
came of it. Philosophy was pressing, and as a contrast to all the theology I had
been through, I felt a need to immerse myself in Greek philosophy. As I wrote a
few years later in my doctoral \textit{vita}: ``Classical antiquity came before
me in a fresher and clearer light precisely by contrast with the scholastic
period I seemed to myself to have lived through; I took real pleasure in reading
its historians, its poets, and soon its philosophers above all. I thereby won a
deep conviction of the independent significance of science and of humane life.''
Here too Kierkegaard's guidance was constantly at work. He had clearly maintained
the Socratic-Platonic conception of thought and life as a power of the spirit, of
which one had to give oneself an account before one could with full right and
clear consciousness come to a standpoint that was really higher. I now read the
Greek philosophical works in sequence, and in the following years this became my
principal study. In my teaching I exchanged, some years later, history and Danish
for Latin (in the middle forms) and Greek (in the upper forms), and there came in
this way a certain connection between my life at school and my life in the study.
Besides Mariboe's School I also taught at the ``Lyceum'' and, for a short time, at
``Bohr's School''. I was very fond of teaching, especially in the upper forms, and
here I have won friends who have shown me faithful devotion even after their
schoolyears.

\section*{The Controversy over Faith and Knowledge}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The Controversy over Faith and Knowledge}
\label{sec:troogviden}

In the winter of 1865--66 a discussion was carried on in the press about Rasmus
Nielsen's doctrine of faith and knowledge as two wholly different principles
which, precisely on account of their difference, were supposed to be practically
and personally reconcilable. I had been led to share this view through my own
personal development in religious respects, as well as through my philosophical
studies. This last requires a short explanation.

Here at home, around 1860, Hegel's philosophy was still regarded as a high point
of thought. A magnificent attempt had been made there to show that, just as in
the human mind a single thought is understood only by its relation to other
thoughts, so in nature, in history, in the world of experience as a whole, no
phenomenon and no event is isolated, but everything is bound up with everything
else in the most intimate way. As the one thought proceeds from the other, so the
one phenomenon of experience from the other. And Hegel held that it was possible
to develop the mutual connection between thoughts in such a way that it should at
the same time come to express the connection between things. From this sprang his
great confidence in pure speculation. Human thought could not be something
isolated in the world; its laws had to be the laws of the world.

Here at home this doctrine had been the object of searching criticism by Sibbern
and Kierkegaard. The intimations these thinkers gave of a more critical and more
empirical philosophy were not attended to. For the time being the position was
that both science and religion, each for itself, claimed to give an absolute
solution to the riddles of existence. Here it stood for Hegel in such a way that
the religious representations contained, in the form of image, the same thing
that philosophy set forth in the pure form of thought. Trusting in this view,
many Hegelians believed themselves to be in good agreement with the doctrine of
the Church, and Rasmus Nielsen for a time was of their number. But there came a
great reversal in his view under the influence of Kierkegaard's writings. He broke
with his former allies, particularly Martensen, and now maintained the absolute
independence of religion over against science. They now came to stand as two
different worlds, and man had to live in both of them. This view was attacked
from two sides. Theology continued to claim to be a science, and saw a danger in
religion's being consigned to the world of the purely personal and subjective.
From the opposite side it was above all Georg Brandes who came forward, in
various newspaper articles and later in a small work, \textit{Dualismen i vor
nyeste Filosofi} [Dualism in Our Most Recent Philosophy]. I took part in the
quarrel in a very clumsily shaped little work, \textit{Filosofi og Teologi}
[Philosophy and Theology] (1866), which was in the main a report of the
discussion that had been carried on, after the appearance of Martensen's
\textit{Dogmatik} (1849), about the relation between philosophy and theology. My
own relation to the whole matter I expressed some years later in my doctoral
\textit{vita} thus: ``I have always conceived this view [Rasmus Nielsen's
conception of faith and knowledge] more from the sceptical and negative side,
more as a means to a personal solution of the difficulties, than as a positive
doctrine in its own right.'' That is to say, I was more a Kierkegaardian than a
Nielsenian. Brøchner later drew express attention to the opposition between
Kierkegaard's standpoint and Nielsen's. And this opposition became (as I have
shown in \textit{Danske Filosofer}) more and more pronounced in the course of
Nielsen's continued development of his thoughts, until at last (in his work
\textit{Om Aandsdannelse} [On the Formation of the Mind], 1877) he wholly
disavowed Kierkegaard and in effect conceded the case to Kierkegaard's
theological opponents.

For my part the matter stood thus, that I was on the way out of theology, and
that what now mattered was what conception of science, and of a personal outlook
on life, I could arrive at. A new period of learning now began for me. What
mattered was to obtain a foundation for my further development, both in
scientific and in purely personal respects. Of this, of course, I was not
conscious at the time; but when I look back I find an involuntary striving after
a footing. Some eight years passed before such a footing was reached. I had to
learn the peculiar character of the different main problems, and thereby to
understand both their connection and their independence. In a short sketch for a
lecture course in the autumn of 1874 on ``Introduction to Philosophy'' were
contained the ground-lines of everything I have since presented at greater or
less length.

%%  Prize Essay, Doctorate, and Paris  [Ch. II paragraphs 40, 41]
%% ============================================================
\section*{Prize Essay, Doctorate, and Paris}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Prize Essay, Doctorate, and Paris}
\label{sec:paris}

But I anticipate. --- My studies in Greek philosophy gathered, at the point at
which I here stand, more and more around a definite task, which I treated in a
work, \textit{Den antike Opfattelse af den menneskelige Villie} [The Ancient
Conception of the Human Will], that I defended in 1870 for the doctorate in
philosophy. Some years earlier I had answered the University's philosophical
prize question (for 1867--68) and won the gold medal. That question, too,
concerned the human will, in that it called for an investigation of the dispute
about freedom of the will which was conducted in its day (1824 and following) in
our literature between the forensic physician Howitz, Sibbern, A.~S.\ Ørsted and
others. I am not satisfied with the solution I intimated, and the treatise had,
in the judgment of the examiners (Nielsen and Brøchner), its essential value in
its searching characterisation of the various standpoints that had asserted
themselves, of which those of Howitz and Sibbern especially had interested me.

From this time on I came into closer relations with Brøchner. When I had won the
gold medal he advised me to travel abroad, and my father then made me a present
of a journey. I wavered somewhat as to where I should go. Alongside my other work
I had sought to orient myself with regard to the development of philosophy in
Germany after Hegel's time, and here the Göttingen philosopher Hermann Lotze had
particularly attracted my attention. I considered the possibility of studying
under him at Göttingen. But I judged that I could get to know this subtle thinker
and gifted writer quite as well through his books as through his lectures, and
that a stay in a small German university town would not be able to enrich me in
any areas beyond the purely scientific. I thought I should get a more many-sided
profit from spending a winter in Paris, where not only could one study, but where
art and literature and a pulsing metropolitan life offered possibilities of a
more many-sided education. I spent a very rich winter in Paris (1868--69),
attended lectures (among others by Taine), worked at my dissertation, was a
constant visitor at museums and theatres, and used my eyes to observe life in
that centre of the world. Here I came to know other directions of thought than
those which stemmed from Germany and which had hitherto had the greatest
influence on those at home who concerned themselves with philosophy. I became
especially aware of the positivist tendency, represented partly by the great
figure of Comte --- beside, and in contrast to, Hegel the greatest systematic
thinker of the nineteenth century --- and partly by Herbert Spencer's bold but
magnificent generalisation of the concept of development. It was some time,
however, before this tendency came to have a more decisive significance for me. I
have always been slow to turn. Thoughts had to ripen quietly in me before I could
work with them.

\section*{From the Letters of the Engagement Years}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{From the Letters of the Engagement Years}
\label{sec:breve}

30 September 1866. --- If one tries to keep life going on idyllic, contemplative
harmony, one will surely come up against reality and learn that the
reconciliation was only in thought or in imagination \ldots{} That is why I have
become a disciple of Rasmus Nielsen: because he so strongly emphasises the
opposition between faith and knowledge. They are, after all, two wholly different
states of the soul. Faith is an ``unaltered disposition'', but knowledge belongs
to the realm of contemplation.

\begin{center}[\,\ldots\,]\end{center}

1 March 1867. --- You said once at Christmas that I never wrote anything about my
studies \ldots{} So far as I can come to clarity with myself, I am convinced that
it is the one right thing I am doing when I strive after a more comprehensive and
more deeply penetrating knowledge and understanding in philosophical respects. It
must lead to a goal which for me personally will be of great importance; I feel
so surely that my life would become unsteady and wavering if I turned these
questions away and let them stand in an indeterminate fog. In that respect I
harbour no doubt, although the goal of course often seems to hide itself from me,
and I seem to myself so powerless and so wretched. Whether I shall succeed in
reaching such a result that it should also have some significance outwardly is,
on the other hand, another question. One cannot, after all, get further than
one's ability reaches. But do you not agree with me that the first consideration
is the more important? (There followed a discussion of practical prospects; I
imagine myself taking the doctorate, working as a teacher at a school, and one
day taking over one of the private Latin schools here in town.) To become a
university teacher is something that in our small country is a piece of sheer
good fortune, quite apart from the demands it makes on talent and personality.

\begin{center}[\,\ldots\,]\end{center}

7 November 1867. --- It may perhaps interest you to hear in what direction my
reading is chiefly going. Since the question of faith and knowledge, which
tormented me when I was studying theology and which has ever since presented
itself to me anew, led me to a closer occupation with the history of philosophy, I
have gradually become convinced that without having grasped Greek thought in its
characteristic main forms one would not have the right conception of what the
matter really turns on. For the Greeks had attained the highest completion of
spiritual development that could be reached under purely human conditions. With
Christianity, faith came into the world and new forces were set in motion; but
everything we have in the way of human forms of culture points back in its main
features to the Greeks. For half a year now, therefore, I have been occupied with
Plato and Aristotle, and if I succeed in carrying through my plan I shall one day
use a subject from this field for a doctoral dissertation. Alongside this I busy
myself with history and Icelandic, which I need for my teaching.

14 November 1867. --- Lately I have read a good deal of Shakespeare aloud,
especially on Sunday evenings, and have also --- which is a great pleasure ---
heard Poul Kierkegaard read. About a fortnight ago he, Troels Lund and I sat here
one evening and read Shakespeare until it was almost one o'clock, without our
knowing it.

24 November 1867. --- A change that has taken place in me this autumn consists in
my no longer being able to agree with Rasmus Nielsen, to whom, as you know, I had
formerly attached myself on the whole. Partly a wavering in himself, partly a
striking criticism by Professor Brøchner, partly and above all a renewed testing
of the matter itself (not least in conversations with Poul Kierkegaard), have
shown me what is untenable in his view. Yet I have not thereby been brought
closer to the ecclesiastical \ldots{} On the contrary, it has at the same time
dawned on me that on several points I held opinions which had no true root in me,
but stuck fast as something external and handed down. Christianity will --- of
this I am fully and firmly convinced --- always be the highest truth for me, the
one in which alone I can find the riddles of life solved. But only what I can
personally experience and make my own can be truth for me; and I have come to the
conviction that whatever one supposes oneself to possess beyond that rests on
unconscious hypocrisy. The view whose untenability I have now come to see was of
course not without significance; it was a stage of development that had to be
passed through.

%% ============================================================
%%  Privatdocent: Science and Personality  [Ch. II paragraphs 67, 68, 69]
%% ============================================================
\section*{Privatdocent: Science and Personality}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Privatdocent: Science and Personality}
\label{sec:privatdocent}

In the spring semester of 1871 I began to lecture as \textit{privatdocent} at the
University. My effort to orient myself within contemporary philosophy led to the
publication of two books, \textit{Filosofien i Tyskland efter Hegel} [Philosophy
in Germany after Hegel] (1872) and \textit{Den engelske Filosofi i vore Dage}
[English Philosophy in Our Day] (1874). Through a critical testing of English
philosophy, especially as represented by Mill and Spencer, I was led to see the
significance of a thought which had lain behind Kierkegaard's philosophising and
had still earlier been consciously brought forward by Kant: namely the thought of
the unifying, unity-seeking character of the life of human consciousness. From
this I later derived the hypothesis I laid at the basis of my \textit{Psykologi}.
In the autumn of 1874 I gave a course of lectures on ``Introduction to
Philosophy'', in which, from the standpoint thus won, I characterised the
different philosophical problems. From this time onward it became gradually clear
to me that it is philosophy's chief task to show how that fundamental peculiarity
of the human soul displays itself in the various domains of human thought. My
philosophy, if I may use the word, became a doctrine of problems --- that is, of
tasks determined by the interaction between the manifoldness of observations and
the unity-seeking need and capacity. No closed system could come out of this. On
the contrary, through new observations and new energy of thought new tasks must
constantly present themselves. All that matters is to show that everything which
becomes a problem for the human spirit bears that spirit's stamp and is
determined by its nature, whether or not the problem can be solved once and for
all. It has been a fruitful working hypothesis for me, and for over forty years I
have sought to apply it to a series of questions. I have found that this point of
view was satisfying not only in the intellectual respect (though people have
sometimes found it too sceptical or too subjective), but that here too there was
a possibility of that harmony between science and personality which I had so long
sought. For personally as well, it is a matter of bringing a whole out of the
manifold of experiences, impulses and thoughts that make up the content of our
inner life. The sharp opposition between science and personality thereby falls
away. Science is itself a personal labour, sprung from the innermost need of
personality. And on the other hand there is nothing in personal life which could
not be made the object of scientific consideration --- at all events of
historical and psychological investigation.

Given this relation between science and personality, it becomes both unnecessary
and impossible to entertain any fear that science should lead to results harmful
to personal life. And just as little is there ground for lyrical proclamations
that there is nothing above science and nothing beside it. Science will not let
itself be cowed; but it is too serious a matter to be the object of a lyricism
that is not backed, point for point, by work.

For my part it became from this time on more and more clear that what was to
support me in life could not be anything outside me, but had to be something that
made itself known within me --- in the very best and clearest thoughts I could
win, in the very best and purest striving that could lay claim to me. What
mattered was not a vision but an eye that could see --- a central power within
me, built upon experience of life and upon the testing of thought --- an inward
home, a chapel built up out of everything that could give life value. The measure
of truth and of value had to be drawn from our own innermost being, and it is in
reality from there that science draws it, without always being conscious of the
fact. Every image, every form, every concept by whose help we are able to
interpret this innermost thing, which is at the same time the highest, and by
which we try to make it an object for ourselves, can have only symbolic
significance; and here lies the boundary between scientific thinking and poetic
analogising.

%%  Hans Brøchner  [Ch. II paragraphs 78, 79, 80, 81]
%% ============================================================
\section*{Hans Brøchner}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Hans Brøchner}
\label{sec:brochner}

When, after this Jutland excursion, I turn back to my life in Copenhagen, there is
one figure that stands out for me in my recollection of the years around 1870. It
is Hans Brøchner. I had come into connection with him in the summer of 1867, when,
having heard me speak as an opponent at a dissertation defence, he sent me a kind
and encouraging greeting. From that time on I visited him often. I received advice
from him regarding my study of the history of philosophy, and when I had written a
critical review of one of his books, he sent me his counter-remarks and asked me to
come one evening and discuss them with him. In the following years, until his death
(1875), I visited him regularly once a month in his solitary home, where he lived
as a widower with his two children. He associated with none of his contemporaries,
with the exception of his friend from youth, Privy Councillor Vedel. Of the younger
men, only Georg Brandes besides myself came to him, after his closest disciple, the
amiable and gifted Kristian Møller, had died. When one visited him it was as a rule
not philosophical subjects that came up. In his solitude he felt a desire to hear
news of the academic world and of the world at large. I still see him sitting in
his rocking chair, or walking up and down the floor with his characteristic long
and decided strides. The unusually handsome face could then light up with goodwill
and interest. Often he spoke of his travels, or of his young wife who had died
early. Of Costanza, the young Italian woman he had been in love with in his youth,
he never spoke to me; it was only when I published his letters that I came to know
this episode of his life. In the letters it appears, as he describes it, like a
novel full of feeling. It seems to have been a confessor's intervention that
prevented his bringing Costanza up to the North. It would surely have been a risky
experiment too. --- When one rose to take one's leave, he became particularly
lively and had many things to ask and to talk about; it was as though he were
reluctant to let go of the few links that bound him to the outside world.

He was a philosophical hermit. The circle of thoughts he had made his own was his
spiritual home. He had not much capacity for communicating himself in free form,
varying his expressions according to situations and personalities, and he had once
and for all gathered his ideas into definite, very abstract forms. But both in
lectures and in conversation there could come a vibration into his voice and his
expression, a sign of the deep feeling that lay behind and bore witness to the
connection of the thought with the whole personality. He was a knight of thought,
the last representative among us of that idealism which, confident that the
innermost of existence could find, and had found, expression in the form of
thought, found in this same form the expression of personal life's highest
aspiration. For those who came after him in the field of philosophy the problems
have become more composite and more manifold. In one of his last lectures Brøchner
declared himself an adherent of ``the idealist philosophy which Germany has the
honour of having brought forth'', and there have hardly been many in whom that
philosophy's chief thinker so thoroughly permeated and ennobled the personality as
in him. The image of ``the quiet professor'', as he has been called, has followed
me on my way and reminded me what value the life of thought can have, beyond the
purely intellectual domain as well, when there is seriousness in the work of
thought. His friendship was an honour and an encouragement to me, and it was with
deep emotion that I took leave of him on his deathbed. It was the day before he
died that I saw him for the last time. He spoke with complete calm about his
departure, and settled various matters with me. When he bade me farewell and
thanked me for my friendship, I was more overcome than he. His housekeeper
afterwards related that he had said quite calmly, when I had gone: ``Take that
glass away, for Dr Høffding's tears are in it.''

A little myth formed itself, when he was dead, about his last moments. He died on
17 December (1875) towards the afternoon --- that is, in the darkest season. One of
the last things he said was: ``We must have light in here.'' In the period
immediately after his death I heard from several quarters that he was supposed to
have cried out for light, and there were those who added that it was no wonder that
\textit{that} man cried out for light! --- The myth seems, however, to have died a
natural death. For a time it took some nourishment from what happened at his
funeral. There was at that time no possibility of a purely civil burial. He had
wished to be buried from the University, and that a colleague should speak; but if
that could not be done, he had named a particular clergyman who had spoken over his
young friend Kristian Møller and had on that occasion shown much tact. But the
clergyman in question seems to have misunderstood the situation and read more into
the deceased's very conditional wish than was warranted. I thought of correcting the
misunderstanding, but gave it up when Brøchner's oldest friend, Privy Councillor
Vedel, firmly advised against it.

In the preface to my edition of the correspondence of Brøchner and Molbech, and in
\textit{Danske Filosofer}, I have given a characterisation of Brøchner as a
personality and as a thinker; and my work \textit{Den nyere Filosofis Historie} [The
History of Modern Philosophy] I have dedicated ``to the memory of my teacher and
friend''.

%% ============================================================
%%  The Circle of Friends in Copenhagen  [Ch. II paragraph 85]
%% ============================================================
\section*{The Circle of Friends in Copenhagen}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The Circle of Friends in Copenhagen}
\label{sec:vennekreds}

In Copenhagen we associated first and foremost with Julius Petersen and his wife
Oliva, née Munch. He now practised in Nørrebro and quickly acquired a large
practice, not least in the poor quarters. He was restlessly active, and had already
begun his studies in the history of medicine. When he came home from sick-visits in
poor homes he could be indignant at the luxury he thought he saw in their very
modest dwellings, and he could sit sunk in dejection and discontent. But suddenly a
quite different mood could come over him, and often, when we thought the barometer
was low, there would come an invitation to take part in a little party. Against his
restlessness and his many swings there was good use for Oliva's level-headedness,
clear understanding and firm will. As the flock of children quickly grew, there was
enough for her to do. And she always kept her house running well, so that it even
became a gathering place for his friends. Here we met with Kristian Arentzen, Viggo
Hørup and Holger Drachmann and their wives, and in many long speeches at the festive
supper table the many questions of the time were discussed. --- Troels Lund I had
made the acquaintance of in my student years. From philology he had gone over to
theology, and after his examination he took up, as I did, studies in Greek
philosophy, and defended (1871) an interesting dissertation on Socrates. Later he
went over to historical studies, occasioned by an appointment at the National
Archives (the Privy Archives, as it was then called). Troels Lund's fine, harmonious
personality was a pleasure to be with, and he and his lively wife made their home a
place we gladly came to. And in those days one could also have the pleasure of being
surprised by a visit; it was so agreeable when Lund and his wife came and settled in
for the evening, making do with whatever the house could provide. --- Heegaard's home,
too, calls up many good recollections. He was an opponent at my doctoral defence, and
from that time we came to stand close to one another. Having broken with Rasmus
Nielsen, he remained in a searching state, absorbed in eager study of Kant and of the
newer English philosophers; he also occupied himself a great deal with mathematics. As
his financial circumstances were straitened, he had to take on exhausting extra work.
Among other things he wrote anonymously a series of novels in which he was strongly
influenced by Dickens. For some years they found quite a large readership. He also
took on office work for a publisher for a time. Heegaard was, in his vigorous period,
open and jovial, full of mockery for dogmatists of every kind; the clergy he liked to
call bonzes. He perhaps declaimed a little too much against dogmatism instead of
immersing himself actively in positive work. But it was fruitful and encouraging to be
in his company. And in his home his wife had a refreshing effect by her clear and
cheerful bearing in the most various circumstances.

%%  Georg Brandes  [Ch. II paragraphs 110, 111, 112, 113, 114]
%% ============================================================
\section*{Georg Brandes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Georg Brandes}
\label{sec:brandes}

Georg Brandes and I had been opponents in the controversy over faith and knowledge.
Later we often met at the house of Troels Lund's father. When he and his brother had
founded the journal \textit{Det nittende Aarhundrede} [The Nineteenth Century], I
became a contributor and thereby came into a closer and, for me, very beneficial
connection with him.

He invited me one evening after he had married, and he and his wife also visited my
home. I have always followed him with admiration in his works of literary criticism. He
has, for me as for so many others, opened new poetic and literary worlds, and by his
capacity for searching characterisation and analysis he has cast light on so many of
literature's great figures. I felt myself very different from him in temperament. I took
everything more heavily and more awkwardly, and I lacked the self-confidence which is the
condition of bold action. What I have been able to accomplish is owing to long and
untiring work, and my tasks lay further from the great public. Besides, I have generally
held the view that whatever is to be altered or cleared out of the way in the world of
thought or of society can first be recognised once the right thing has been found. It is
difficult to find the right thing, but when it is found it becomes clear what the false
is. And the forms in which men's need for support and anchorage in life comes to rest can
be altered only when that need itself is altered; but such alteration takes place very
slowly, if it is to be thorough. Here too it is truth that shows what is false,
positivity that grounds negativity. As Spinoza says: truth is the sign both of itself and
of falsity (\textit{verum est index sui et falsi}). And Comte maintained that one
displaces only what one replaces. This way of looking at things meant that I stood
differently towards religious and moral polemic than Brandes did. But his criticism has in
many ways benefited my own activity, in that it awakened in many people a need for a
further orientation in the domain of thought. I have certainly seen, in not a few cases,
how the view arose that it was enough to have brisk and reckless opinions, without one's
needing to mind that philosophy demanded a searching investigation.

What I am not least grateful to Brandes for, from the time I am speaking of here, the
seventies, is the advice he gave me on the occasion of the contributions I sent to his
journal. Thus he wrote to me on 7 June 1875: ``Allow me, not so much as editor as in the
capacity of a sympathetic fellow-labourer, to put a couple of questions to you. Have you
ever made the experiment of reading what you send to press aloud to some man who has not
attended a learned school? Have you read it aloud to a lady who was not particularly
schooled in philosophy? Have you put a mark against the passages he or she did not
understand, and tried to rewrite them in other words? I venture to say that you have not
made this attempt. But why not?'' And then he complains particularly of a passage in an
article I had submitted to the journal, which was undeniably expressed in a very
unpopular way. I found the complaint just; unfortunately it probably also strikes much of
what I have written since, although I really did set myself to write as accessibly as
possible. But there is, after all, a difference between philosophy and literary history;
and besides, it has been my lot to develop slowly and to come late to clarity. I could
have waited to write until everything could be expressed clearly (if not for those who
have no presuppositions at all); that would perhaps have been no loss to literature. But I
have myself always learned much by attempting to present my thoughts and the knowledge I
had so far acquired. And I had in Brøchner an example of what it can lead to when one
waits too long before writing; he often lamented that he had wanted to wait with his
compositions until he had completely worked through all his subjects, and when that time
seemed to him to have come he was nearly fifty, and had lived himself wholly into the
abstract expressions handed down from Hegelian philosophy, which in themselves might have
done good service as short formulas for his solitary thinking. --- Yet I have often since
been surprised at how wide the circles are that many of my books have reached.

When in 1876 I had published my little book \textit{Om Grundlaget for den humane Etik} [On
the Foundation of Humane Ethics], to which I shall return in another connection, Brandes
wrote to me (24 October 1876): ``You are, though on another foundation, the
`transfigured' Brøchner, and it would certainly have been a great joy to Brøchner had he
lived to see this book of yours. \ldots{} I congratulate you also on the form of your
book. You have gained extraordinarily here in freedom and mobility. You once said a
despondent word to me about dryness in your style, in which you were far too severe with
yourself. \ldots{} There is in my opinion still too little temperament in your style; you
do not yet write sufficiently with your whole personality, the whole man. But you are on
the way to doing so.'' --- For the rest, Brandes recalls in this letter the inscription on
the papers of the Committee of Public Safety: ``Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité, ou la
mort!'' --- and he thinks that I can hardly ever have been young enough to say this. In
that he is quite right. Yet it jarred a little on my ears when he added: ``You must have
been born to a certain degree old.'' Of himself he then says: ``I am afraid that I was
once very young.'' ---

The relation into which I had come with Brandes was a source of much joy and encouragement
to me. After Brøchner's death I felt lonely, and since Brandes united with his great
artistic sense an understanding of philosophical investigations, his letters were always
welcome to me. Some years later he wrote to me from Berlin: ``I am very glad to stand on
such friendly terms with you; there is nothing I would rather do than live in brotherly
understanding with all my contemporaries in the North who inquire honestly.'' What was
here so warmly expressed found a complete echo on my side. And this feeling did not
disappear in me even when, a good many years later, it was put to the test by a serious
and sharp opposition between our views on an essential point.
