\begin{center}\large I.\end{center}

By the principle of personality I understand the thought that the human being,
at every point and in every respect, is to be treated---by others and by
himself---as an end, never merely as a means. Even where the human being
serves---serves other human beings, serves society, serves a cause, an
idea---he is at the same time to be an end himself, in that the serving ought
to lead to the development and ennoblement of his own being, of its individual
distinctiveness. It is an ideal that is set up in this principle, which was
first formulated with full clarity and definiteness by Immanuel Kant (1785). In
the Nordic countries it was asserted with great emphasis by Erik Gustaf Geijer
in Sweden (1842), from whom the name ``principle of personality'' derives.
Whereas Kant set up this principle in opposition partly to the principle of
% --- p. 6 ---
\opage{6}%
authority, partly to an external morality of utility and happiness, Geijer set
it against the romantic-conservative ``historical school,'' which laid weight
solely on institutions and traditions, such as these had once come to develop,
and he found more and more that everything he had worked for in literature, in
politics and in religion was contained in this principle. In Denmark the
principle was stated, especially in the religious field, at about the same time
(1846) by F.~C. Sibbern and Søren Kierkegaard.---If we followed the history of
the principle further back, we should come on the one side to Christianity and
the weight it laid on the individual soul, on the other to the Socratic
insistence on the significance of self-knowledge and self-appraisal.

The argument for the principle is very different in its various
representatives. To this I shall return later, when I shall at the same time
examine whether this principle can be proved conclusively at all. Here I want
to try to set out everything that it contains. It is first and foremost set up
as an ethical and religious principle. But I believe that it also contains
points of view of great importance
% --- p. 7 ---
\opage{7}%
for a right understanding of the relation of science to life. It has an
intellectual, not merely an ethical and religious, significance, and I shall
examine it from all three of these sides. What Geijer said of his time, that it
``is a time that suffers from its own thoughts,'' in that ``the inner world
totters in its foundations, in its faith, its conviction, its knowledge''---and
that ``the fundamental and mother thought that here wants out and forces its
way, and in whose birth-pangs the world trembles,'' is precisely the principle
of personality,\footnote{\textit{Samlade Skrifter} (1853) I 6, p.~287.}---this
surely still holds to this day. The crisis is not over; it has only taken
somewhat different forms than in Geijer's time. Geijer's words aptly say that
here is a thought that has worked its way forward involuntarily, but that can
come into its full right only by becoming the object of clear consciousness.

When we now begin with the intellectual significance of the principle of
personality, we meet the objection that in science
% --- p. 8 ---
\opage{8}%
the point is precisely to look away from our personality, its striving, its
pleasure and pain, its hope and its fear. Ought we not precisely to divest
ourselves of our personality if we want to be investigators, if we want to find
the great laws of existence, which hold for each and all? In science we are
led into a common world. But every personality has its own little world,
different for each, like the world of dreams in contrast to the waking reality
common to all. Can personality here demand more than to be allowed to live in
the world of its dreams, so long as science does not draw this, too, under the
strict jurisdiction of its research?---

I go for a moment into this analogy between dream and reality on the one side,
personality and science on the other. Psychology shows us that the same laws
hold for consciousness in dreaming and in the waking state. In both there
appears a need and a capacity to sum up and order all experiences, all
sensations, feelings and thoughts. In dreams this happens in a more erratic and
seemingly irregular way---now with genius, now stupidly. In the waking state it
happens more according to
% --- p. 9 ---
\opage{9}%
definite theoretical and practical points of view (although here too both
stupidity and genius may occur). The relation between personality and science
is analogous. It is a kindred need and a kindred capacity that are at work in
both. We speak of personality only where a striving for connection in the
mental life appears, a striving to gather all psychical elements and lead them
in definite directions. Continuity and concentration are the distinguishing
marks of personality. Science is only a particular, specially shaped form of
this characteristic. Insofar as there is an opposition between personality and
science, it should be established that it has its place within personality,
---that it concerns the relation between personality as a whole and one of its
special expressions of life.

One of the chief tasks of philosophy is precisely to investigate how and why
scientific consciousness develops as a special form of personal life.
Philosophical reflection seeks to answer the question how the nature of the
human mind expresses itself, both in the work that leads to a great common
picture of the world,
% --- p. 10 ---
\opage{10}%
and in the work that perhaps acquires significance only by bringing harmony and
order into the individual's own little world. This little world need not be a
world of dreams; here the analogy mentioned above cannot simply be applied; in
any case there is a special problem here, to which we shall return later.

It is critical philosophy that has maintained with the greatest energy that the
work of thought in science cannot be concluded, and cannot be understood in its
full significance, without regard to the nature and conditions of conscious
beings. Every objective truth has a subjective foundation, even if one cannot
infer this truth from the subjective foundation in and by itself.

The historical origin of science is to be found where the human being has had
the experience that she can reach her ends only by quite definite ways. She
would rather reach them immediately. Only when forced by experience does she go
by detours. And she then stands face to face with a connection to which she must
bow. Here occurs her first meeting with the necessity that science uncovers at
one point after the
% --- p. 11 ---
\opage{11}%
other. Science demands resignation. Here the human being thinks in order to
live. The work of thought, and thereby of personality, is here only a means. But
it is a means that can itself become an end, by becoming the object of immediate
interest. It becomes an independent task to come to know the great connection
within which personal life is lived, to find an order of things more
comprehensive than the individual's own little world. And then the human being
can live in order to think. She grows with the greater ends she thus sets
herself. Personality becomes richer the greater the circle of experiences it
embraces, provided only that it can gather them into a harmonious unity, or at
least divine and seek such a unity. It is easy enough to concentrate oneself
when there is only a poor and limited content to sum up, when no great
oppositions, no wealth of experiences, lay claim to the psychical energy. The
world of personality must be widened if this energy is rightly to come to light.

Science and its history first become comprehensible when there is an inner
connection between scientific
% --- p. 12 ---
\opage{12}%
work and the whole life and activity of personality. Scientific work presupposes
an energy and an enthusiasm, an \textit{amor intellectualis}, which is not
possible without an inner connection between science and personality. And such
a connection in turn presupposes that it is no alien power to which personality
bows when through research it is led to acknowledge a great connection that
points far beyond its own little world. The longing to find truth by the way
that science points out is only a special form of the longing for agreement with
oneself amid all the manifold and changing experiences---the longing of which
the very being of personality consists. And even where personality keenly feels
the opposition between its own little world and the immeasurable world,
reaching beyond all human works and ends, to which science opens our eyes, it
belongs precisely to the nobility of personality, as well as to its duty, to
find by its own strength, by straining the energy of its own thought, the great
laws that condition its existence and its values. Personality is purified by
seeing its
% --- p. 13 ---
\opage{13}%
existence conditioned and limited by the great connection of things.

Not all sides of personality are called upon to the same degree by scientific
work. In his research the human being must start from definite points of view,
follow definite paths, develop his life of thought according to certain definite
methods, which are indeed special shapings of personal life in general, but which
in the course of mankind's long labor of thought have proved indispensable.
Science works with certain fundamental concepts or fundamental predicates, which
of old are called categories, and which together make up an armor suited both to
personality and to the resistance that the work of thought has to
overcome.\footnote{Cf.\ on this \textit{Den menneskelige Tanke, dens Former og
dens Opgaver} (1910), Chap.~III.} Personality, insofar as it appears in this
armor, has sometimes been called ``the pure I,'' in contrast to ``the empirical
I,'' which shows us personality in the whole of its individual reality, with all
its experiences, ideas, wishes and drives. The pure I is the I of the solitude
of thought, of the study
% --- p. 14 ---
\opage{14}%
and the laboratory, the I that works strictly with ``the categories.'' But it is
only a particular shaping, made necessary by the division of labor, of this same
I that manifests itself in ideas, wishes and drives. There is no absolute
difference between them, and their mutual relation can appear in countless
shades in different individuals. There are investigators who are transformed
into quite different people when they step out of the solitude of thought into
ordinary life. But that is an imperfection that points to an unresolved
disharmony in personal life itself.

On this point stands the conflict of our day between criticism and pragmatism.
The former is inclined (already in its founder, Immanuel Kant) to regard ``the
pure I,'' the sum of the forms of investigating thought (the categories), as
given once and for all, independent of all experience and history (including the
history of science itself). The latter is inclined (both in the form Ernst Mach
and in the form William James has given it) to regard the forms and
presuppositions that are necessary for scientific work as conditioned merely by
external tasks and
% --- p. 15 ---
\opage{15}%
practical interests, without standing in any inner connection with human nature
itself. In my view this contradiction falls away when one examines more closely
the nature and development of the particular forms of thought. In my book on
``Den menneskelige Tanke'' I have worked in this direction, and have thereby been
strengthened in the conviction that in science the human being stands neither
merely as a means for the application of certain pure forms nor merely for the
satisfaction of tasks given from without.

Since science thus in its origin points back to human personality and appears as
a particular form of the work that every personality has to perform in order to
assert and develop itself, its personal character becomes visible from another
and more individual side at the conclusion of scientific work. An absolute
conclusion, a conclusion once and for all, is not possible, since life and
experience always go on. But every individual investigator shall and must stop
at a definite point, where inner or outer causes prevent him from getting
further. And then he will naturally seek a survey, a
% --- p. 16 ---
\opage{16}%
summing-up, a rounding-off. He will at the same time attempt a glimpse into the
unexplored, if not unexplorable, regions, as one throws light into the darkness
with an electric searchlight. Here it is not only ``the pure I'' that is at
work; the other sides of personality will cooperate to a greater or lesser
degree. One sees it, for example, in the attempts of natural scientists to set
their results in relation to their whole view of the world. And one sees it when
the historical investigator passes from the critical examination of the sources
to the writing of history; this demands a rounding-off in which the personality
of the investigator asserts itself more than during the critical work. One sees
it still more in philosophy, when it passes from criticism to system, from
analysis to synthesis.

Philosophical systems ought never to have wanted to be more than individual
roundings-off. Rounding-off can be justified even if conclusion is impossible.
Goethe characterized his research by the principle ``Nie geschlossen, oft
gerundet!'' And in the rounding-off the individual character ought to be
expressly acknowledged. The really significant systems do not lose by this, but
precisely thereby gain a special interest. Even the system
% --- p. 17 ---
\opage{17}%
that is often regarded as the most objective and impersonal of all---Spinoza's
---proves on closer examination to be pervaded by the personality of its founder;
it is the bond that unites the many series of thoughts into a unity. Through the
whole course of thought one feels the master's fearless consistency, his great
resignation and his inward absorption in the work of thought, which for him was
a religion. It is not least this that makes many turn back again and again, and
with ever new gain, to this work of art of thought.

The interest that the history of philosophy offers rests to a great extent on
this personal element. The history of philosophy can be regarded as a great
dialogue, whose particular speeches come from personalities of research, each of
whom, on the foundation of the science and culture of his time, has developed a
type of thought, a more or less essential view of existence. For philosophy its
history has, precisely on account of the principle of personality, greater
significance than for other sciences. In this, as in other respects, it stands
between science and art. And for many its great power of attraction lies
precisely in this.

% --- p. 18 ---
\opage{18}
\begin{center}\large II.\end{center}

A right understanding of science requires that one see in it a fruit of personal
work. This excludes both fear of its results and presumption about them.

But personality is not only the subject of science. It is at the same time
itself one of the objects of science. Science turns to it as to a given matter,
which is to be understood and explained in its nature and in its origin. The
personal factor that cooperates in all science can and ought itself to be made
an object of science. The world is understood through the human being
(\textit{ex analogia hominis}); our knowledge of the world is conditioned by the
faculties and forms of human thought. But the human being is himself a part of
the world, which can be understood only in its connection with the rest of the
world. The human being is therefore understood through the world (\textit{ex
analogia universi}).

As Pascal has said: ``In space the world encompasses
% --- p. 19 ---
\opage{19}%
(\textit{comprend}) me, but in thought I encompass (\textit{comprends}) the
world.''

The two ways of regarding things do not conflict with each other. They are two
points of view that must both be adopted, and that stand in inner interaction.
Human thought, which works at solving the riddles of existence, has itself
developed within existence. The presuppositions that condition and limit our
view of the world themselves have a history. It is not least the necessity of
this double point of view that makes an absolute conclusion of knowledge
impossible. %
% the Swedish prints "möjliggör" (makes possible), which the next sentences
% contradict; translated as "omöjliggör" (makes impossible), as the earlier
% English from Blegvad's Danish also has it
We cannot use both points of view at
once. We cannot stand on our head and on our feet at once; but we can (perhaps)
do first the one and then the other.

Against making personality an object of science an interesting objection has,
however, been raised by one of the finest thinkers of present-day Germany,
Heinrich Rickert, in his work ``Die Grenzen der naturwissenschaftlichen
Begriffsbildung'' (1901--1902). Rickert maintains a sharp opposition between
natural science and history (the science of mind): the former concerns the
universal and general, that which is constantly repeated;---the latter has as
its
% --- p. 20 ---
\opage{20}%
object something that happens only once (\textit{das Einmalige}), individual
events and individual figures. The purely natural-scientific method aims at
finding general concepts and laws under which the individual phenomena can be
subsumed as examples. The historical sciences, on the other hand, stop at
individual totalities. The concepts of natural science are general concepts; the
historical concepts are individual concepts!\footnote{``Von den Individuen gibt
es keine Naturwissenschaft, weil sich nichts Besonderes mehr ihnen unterordnen
lässt, im Vergleich zu dem sie noch als ein allgemeiner Begriff oder als eine
`Natur' aufzufassen wären.'' Über die Grenzen der naturwissenschaftlichen
Begriffsbildung, p.~295.}

Against this view I set the claim that in the world of nature, as well as in
that of mind, general concepts and laws are in the end only means of
understanding the individual and the historical (\textit{das Einmalige}). It is
the pride of science that it can form concepts and laws that hold for countless
individuals and events. The capacity for this was already for Plato, the typical
figure of Greek science, a divine gift that ever anew filled his soul with
admiration. Indeed,
% --- p. 21 ---
\opage{21}%
according to Plato the gods themselves were subordinate to the eternal ideas and
rejoiced to look up to them. But no really given phenomenon, whether in the
world of nature or in that of mind, is exhaustively explained by being regarded
as an example of a general concept or of a general law. To understand natural
phenomena as well as historical phenomena an interplay of laws is required. The
laws are the means of thought which by their cooperation make the origin and
development of a phenomenon comprehensible. The solar system, the earth, the
organism, the personality, society---these are examples of individual
totalities, whose history it is the task of science to write, with the help of
the concepts and laws it is able to find. The history of science clearly shows
us the rising significance of the concept of development. In the end the task
appears of writing the biography of ``the world''---insofar as ``the world'' can
appear as a rounded whole.

It is by no means the case that it is only history that exhibits the single
occurrence (\textit{das Einmalige}). Nor does absolute repetition occur in
nature. No event ever recurs in quite the same way. The
% --- p. 22 ---
\opage{22}%
unchangeableness of nature is only apparent. It is a great world drama (or
perhaps rather an epic) that natural science and history are to interpret
together. In it no scene, no situation, no speech occurs more than once, if one
pays close attention to all particulars, all nuances and accents.

It is a great and lofty ideal that is hereby set for science. But it is
necessary for a right understanding of the significance of science. Natural
science approaches it by means of the great hypotheses that are raised on the
foundation of laws that have been found. The laws always concern simple and
elementary relations; afterwards the task presents itself of how these
relations assert themselves within the wholes, of different kinds and different
degrees, that experience exhibits. Whereas natural science goes from the
elements to the totalities, the science of mind stands more directly before
totalities (events, personalities, societies), and such as do not let
themselves be resolved into elements as easily as the material totalities that
natural science treats. This, however, conditions only a difference of degree,
not a difference of kind.

% --- p. 23 ---
\opage{23}%
It has long been recognized that the more comprehensive our concepts are, the
poorer they are in content, and conversely. But it is not richness of content
that is to be subordinated to the comprehensive abstractions; it is the latter
that are to be used to understand and appreciate the former. The most generally
valid sciences, logic and mathematics, are used (apart from their own
independent interest) as means and presuppositions for the more special
sciences. The difficulties increase and the riddles become more numerous the
nearer we come to the individual totalities. But these always indicate the
ultimate objects and tasks of research.

When it is said that we cannot form proper concepts of the individual
(\textit{das Einmalige}), since there is nothing that can be subsumed under it,
the answer must be that the concept we form of an individual phenomenon, an
event (e.g.\ the French Revolution) or a historical personality (e.g.\ Luther),
includes all the different sides, properties, situations and stages of
development that can contribute to its complete characterization. The
``historical'' concepts too, not only those of natural science,
% --- p. 24 ---
\opage{24}%
then comprise a multiplicity. A distinction must be made between two kinds of
individual concepts. If I think of a historical personality in a definite
situation (e.g.\ Luther at Worms), I can form a concrete individual concept of
him; it has an intuitive character and bears the stamp of the memory image or
the image of fantasy. But no historical personality is exhausted in a single
situation (any more than in a single property or at a single phase of
development). My complete concept of it must correspond to all the situations in
which it appears, and it can then be called a typical individual concept. The
great difficulty with individual concepts is precisely to decide the relation
between the concrete and the typical. Is it the young Luther, he who burned the
papal bull, or is it the old Luther, the organizer and dogmatist, who is to be
decisive for our final concept of Luther? Which of the phases of the French
Revolution is to determine its (typical) concept for us?

The solution surely lies in this, that the typical concept of the individual
states the law according to which the transition takes place
% --- p. 25 ---
\opage{25}%
from the one stage and the one situation to the others. It is the law of change
that gives expression to the essence of an individuality. If this law can be
discovered, the nature of the individual can be found again in each of its
stages, and in the preceding stage one can trace the ones that
follow.\footnote{\textit{Leibniz}, the real founder of the concept of
individuality, already expressed this thought. It is ``la loi de l'ordre,''
``la loi du changement,'' that constitutes the individuality of each being.
Lettre à Basnage 1698. (Opera philos.\ ed.\ Erdmann, p.~151).} Every
psychological and historical account works at finding such a law. Every good
biography at any rate lets us divine it.

Art can show us only the concrete, pictures of individual situations and
actions in which a personality comes to light, not the whole sequence of
pictures that would be needed to form a typical concept. Hence conflicting
interpretations can arise of artistically represented figures, for example of
Hamlet. And yet art is able to carry out individualization better than nature
and history as a rule do.

However precarious it may be to form typical individual concepts, these
% --- p. 26 ---
\opage{26}%
nevertheless appear in the end as the highest task of thought. In order to find
general laws, science must often disregard the fact that everything experience
shows us has an individual character, appears as a whole, be it simple and
elementary. There are many degrees of individuality---all the way from a cloud
in the sky or a heap of sand on the shore to a human personality or a human
society. All too often one meets the idea that nature ``begins'' with a chaos of
elements and forces without quality and definite direction, and that all
totalities and individualities are their products. In our textbooks we begin
with the simple and pass on to the compound, because understanding is most
easily reached in this way. But what we know of the elements, of the simple, is
in fact always gained by analysis of the total connection, by abstraction from
individual phenomena that can vary infinitely.\footnote{Cf., with respect to
the question treated in this section, my presentation of ``totality and
development'' as special categories, ``Den menneskelige Tanke,'' pp.~218--237.}

The consideration set out here acquires a
% --- p. 27 ---
\opage{27}%
special significance when personality becomes an object of science. For
personality is not only the example of an individual totality that lies nearest
to us. It is at the same time the most pronounced example of one, in that it
exhibits the most intimate cooperation of elements and forces that lies within
our experience.

We now turn to psychology, the empirical science of personality, to see what it
can teach us about the nature of personality in itself.

% --- p. 28 ---
\opage{28}
\begin{center}\large III.\end{center}

In all the empirical sciences the decisive definitions come at the end, not at
the beginning. Psychology works its way slowly toward a concept of personality.
All its different methods---observation, description, comparison,
experiment---lead directly or indirectly to this goal. When we already now try to
set up---and in what precedes have presupposed---a concept of personality, this
can be done only by way of hypothesis.

As a characteristic trait of personal beings, unity in all states and
undertakings must first be emphasized. We call a human being a personality in
so much the more pronounced a degree, the more all his thoughts, feelings and
strivings are directed toward one goal,---the more concentration there is in
his spiritual life,---the greater continuity there is in his
% --- p. 29 ---
\opage{29}%
strivings. In spite of all changes and shifting situations a common law, a
stamp of unity, or at least a connection, must appear in him. In the most
exalted way this shows itself when the human being is subject to great
oscillations, caught in strong conflicts, and yet does not in the dark hours
deny what on the heights of life stood before him as true and right.

Personality is maintained---like all mental life---only by an uninterrupted
spiritual work, in which psychical energy manifests itself. It is a constant
struggle to hold together and harmonize a given multiplicity of sensations,
thoughts, feelings, drives and interests, which go in different directions or
stand indifferent to one another. The greater this multiplicity and difference
is, the more spiritual work it takes for the mental life to hold together. And
the more intimate the connection and the concentration are, the greater the
psychical energy that must be spent on this work.

It was this concept that I laid at the foundation when, some thirty years ago, I
worked out my presentation of psychology. It had developed in me
% --- p. 30 ---
\opage{30}%
through criticism of the older English psychology (the so-called ``association
psychology''), which sought to explain the mental life---and thereby
personality---as the product of a mechanical joining together of psychical
elements (independent sensations, ideas, etc.), which would stand in roughly the
same relation to personality as a heap of stones to the house that is built.
The task now became to show that, as long as the mental life persists,
connection and unity are never wholly lacking in it, although these can take
very elementary forms. I relied here especially on the study of mental
illnesses, which proves that mental illness consists above all in a dissolution
of the connection within the mental life, in a weakening of psychical energy.
Later my view was confirmed by Pierre Janet's works, especially by his
description of ``psychasthenia,'' a spiritual weakness characterized by this,
that no psychical content, no psychical elements, have dropped out, but that the
energy for holding together and concentrating is lacking, so that patients who
suffer from this illness are unable to make decisions, to take resolutions. What
is lacking is what Janet aptly calls ``psychical tension''
% --- p. 31 ---
\opage{31}%
(\textit{tension psychologique}), which corresponds to what I call synthesis or
psychical energy.

In the child psychical energy is not yet so developed that a strong and lasting
concentration can become possible. Spiritual development consists not merely in
a manifold content, taken up through experience and tradition, but first and
foremost in the energy that brings about an inner connection. The dream state
is characterized above all by lacking firm connection and consistency in the
content of consciousness. Like the child's consciousness, it has a sporadic
character. In it one idea passes easily into another; the situations change
without this as a rule arousing surprise and without any explanation of the
change being sought. In waking consciousness definite directions are held fast
and followed, and the changes are motivated, or, if motivation seems to be
lacking, they become riddles. Even if there is here only a difference of degree,
since there are, after all, waking dreams too, this difference nevertheless
clearly shows the significance of psychical energy, especially since we have
reason to assume that dream consciousness marks a state between the conscious
and the unconscious.

% --- p. 32 ---
\opage{32}%
It is not, however, only from weakness or lack of development that the
particular elements of the mental life---sensations, thoughts, feelings,
etc.---can appear independently and without closer connection. By attention and
analysis we can also bring out and isolate particular elements and, as far as
possible, consider them each by itself. We must distinguish in order to be able
to apprehend clearly. But such distinctions are more or less artificial. They
are reflections, the work of afterthought, and must not be confused with what
immediate experience offers. The more we are able to immerse ourselves in our
purely immediate and involuntary experiences, the more clearly our mental life
shows itself to flow as a continuous stream, in which no element can be isolated
from the others without being changed in its character. This continuous nature
of immediate psychical experiences has in the most recent time been demonstrated
in a brilliant and forceful way by William James and Henri Bergson.

Finally, experimental psychology too points in the same direction. Its real
founder, Fechner, proved
% --- p. 33 ---
\opage{33}%
that the particular sensations do not arise independently of one another but are
woven into one another, in that after a very strong stimulation of the sense
organs a stronger stimulus than usual is required for a sensation of a certain
strength to arise. The so-called contrast effects, too, testify to the great
connection even in our elementary mental life. The color green appears more
pronounced (``saturated'') after red, which is its complementary color, than
after other colors. The absolute independence of the sensations is only
apparent.

And if we consider the matter from the physiological side, we are led in the
same direction. Whatever the relation between soul and body may be, it remains
certain that the mental life is extraordinarily intimately bound to the nervous
system. But the significance of the latter lies in this, that it is a great
organ of concentration, through which all the parts of the body stand in close
connection and interaction, so that a closed and concentrated behavior of the
organism as a whole thereby becomes possible. And the higher and more
energetically developed the mental life we have reason to assume in a living
being, the greater the role played
% --- p. 34 ---
\opage{34}%
by the nervous system, and the more centralized it is. In the outer, sensible
appearance, too, the stamp of unity of the mental life, and thereby of
personality, manifests itself.

It is only in those beings whose mental life has reached a higher degree of
unity and clarity that we speak of personality. Personal life can appear in an
enormous number of different degrees; to a great extent the difference between
the various individuals rests on such a difference of degree in the intensity
of concentration. Only in the case of human beings does one speak of
personality. The mental life of the animal must, according to all experience,
be assumed to be partly so simple, partly so changeable and incoherent, that it
can most nearly be imagined as analogous to our dream life.

In what precedes we have defined the concept of personality only from the
formal side. We have spoken of energy and concentration, of unity and
connection. But the content too, which is gathered into unity, has its
significance. Two personal beings with the same capacity for concentration can
nevertheless be very different, on account of the different content in which
they are absorbed and around which they gather themselves, and the different
direction in which their energy is released.
% --- p. 35 ---
\opage{35}%
In the real respect personality receives its stamp from embracing a central
content, a ruling interest and striving, which conditions the direction in which
the concentration takes place and the psychical energy is used. Every particular
experience, thought, mood, resolution receives its definite place within the
personality through its relation to a fundamental value, which constitutes the
real distinguishing mark of the individual personality, just as the degree of
energy of concentration constitutes its formal distinguishing mark. The little
world that every personality embraces has, at every time, at every stage of
development, a center, in relation to which all the other elements in personal
life are determined in value and significance. To know oneself is to become
conscious of this central interest, this fundamental value, which constitutes
what one might call the real I. If there were no such constantly active elements
within us, we should be strangers to ourselves. There can be constant elements
of a purely peripheral kind within us; but the deeper self-knowledge
presupposes central elements, and it therefore makes a comic impression when
Holberg's Jeppe
% --- p. 36 ---
\opage{36}%
recognizes himself by his hollow tooth. Such a peripheral element cannot
determine the place of all the other elements in the inner order of the
world.\footnote{On the difference between the formal and the real I, cf.\ my
``Psykologi'' V B, 5.}

In personal life a crisis arises if the central elements change. This can
happen through sudden catastrophes or through long periods of doubt and unrest;
but it can also happen slowly and imperceptibly, so that only afterwards do we
discover that an essential change has taken place with us and within us.
Something can work its way forward within us that quite involuntarily determines
our striving, but of which we become conscious only later. An example that lies
close at hand here is Geijer's relation to the thought he gave the name
principle of personality. It arose in him at a late point in his life, in his
fifty-fifth year, but he then discovered that he had long followed it without
himself knowing it. In a letter he wrote on his last birthday (1847), he speaks
of it in the following way.\footnote{\textit{Samlade Skrifter} I, 1, p.~XXXVIII.}
``There is a fundamental thought in my
% --- p. 37 ---
\opage{37}%
whole life which stands out ever more clearly, and which is not my work; for it
has led me and guided me right up to this day. Should I give it a name, it is
none other than the much disputed principle of personality, whose prophet I have
become almost against my will. By this I mean that I have kept away and warded
off from me everything that could hinder and check the development of my own
innermost being. This has mostly happened without my own cooperation. But this
has now come over me, and it becomes clear to me that what I have fought for in
literature, in politics and in religion is really this my innermost personality
and its right, in the best sense.''

Thus the history of the development of the principle of personality itself
offers an example of a chief point in the nature and conditions of personal
life. We are led to see that personality cannot be said to be exhaustively
determined by what, at a given moment, comes to light in clear consciousness
and with full understanding. Just as it is assumed that in organic life
seemingly sudden changes (mutations) can occur, through which new types can
% --- p. 38 ---
\opage{38}%
arise, so too in the spiritual field changes in the guiding lines can be
prepared by a quiet growth, changes which to the superficial observer may seem
inexplicable, although they have their cause in what has gone on in the
innermost core of the mental life. We have here an important example of the
difference between concrete and typical individual representations that was set
forth in the preceding section. The typical concept of a personality can be
reached only by following it through its different stages of development, and
it is often the later stages that direct attention to the content and
significance of the earlier stages. Descriptive psychology has a large field
here. And it is to be hoped that, when the sense for personal life, its forms
and its phases, develops still more than hitherto, significant work can be done
here. Hitherto dogmatic and doctrinaire conceptions of various kinds have
hindered uninterrupted work in this direction. Descriptive psychology approaches
the boundary between science and art; but through clarity of conception and
through the use of the comparative method it can conquer new ground for
science.

% --- p. 39 ---
\opage{39}
\begin{center}\large IV.\end{center}

From the intellectual significance of the principle of personality I now pass
to its practical significance, which is the original one.

Every principle sets up a demand on our thought or on our will. The principle of
personality makes demands in both directions. I have tried to show what demands
it makes on the life of thought. The practical demand it makes becomes, as I
shall now try to show, of great importance for ethics and the philosophy of
religion.

In Kant and Geijer alike, in Kierkegaard and Sibbern, the principle of
personality is set up in a critical and polemical direction against the
dominion of society and tradition over individual personalities. It demands
their free and independent development. But it nevertheless contains within it
the possibility of letting the
% --- p. 40 ---
\opage{40}%
social as well as the individualistic conception of human life and human action
come into its own.

From a purely social point of view it is emphasized that the individual always
arises within a society. An isolated individual does not exist. The individual
conscience itself, the innermost thing in the human being, is a product of
social life, a social inheritance. The individual virtues are socially
conditioned. And on the other hand the individual, with his longing and his
striving, is vanishingly small in relation to the life of society, which goes
on through countless generations. He is only a drop in the ocean. His happiness,
his knowledge, his conscience acquire a vanishingly small significance compared
with the great and lasting course of development of the race and of society.
And the only measure of the individual's significance lies in the contribution
he is able to make to this course of development.

The individualist answers for his part: where should the significance of society
be sought, if not in the security and support it gives to the development of
individual personalities? The value of a society is measured by the degree to
which it fulfills this task. This
% --- p. 41 ---
\opage{41}%
is the only possible measure, already for the good reason that society consists
of individuals and exists only in and through them.

If the social conception makes the individual a means for society, the
individualistic conception makes society a means for the individual.

This contradiction does not appear as long as life is lived essentially under
the involuntary guidance of instincts and traditions. But every development of
culture comes to a point where the individual no longer feels at one with
society, but where society and its demands stand as mere tradition, mere
authority, perhaps as a purely external power. It is such a time that, in
Geijer's words, suffers from its own thoughts. Then---precisely as a result of
social development---independent personalities have developed; but society does
not yet dare fully to acknowledge their independence, and they cannot
acknowledge the value of society. It is really only in such times that
philosophy arises. Philosophy thereby easily takes on an individualistic
character at its origin. Greek philosophy, the thought of the Renaissance and
the philosophy of the eighteenth century are
% --- p. 42 ---
\opage{42}%
significant examples of this. The philosophy of the nineteenth century
signifies---in both its romantic and its positivist main form---a reaction
against this, just as the great workers' associations too, whose rise is among
the greatest events of that century, have an anti-individualistic character.
But the problem rises again and again, and we have entered the new century with
it.

In this problem a definite distinction must be made between the historical and
the philosophical way of looking at things. It is a fact that the individual
develops within society. His very longing and his striving, his ethical ideas
and the criticism he exercises upon tradition, cannot be understood if one
starts merely from the individual person, thought of as isolated. The
individual is to that extent a product of social causes. And society reaches far
beyond the individual, both into the future and into the past. But does there
follow from this with necessity an obligation for the individual to bow under
the laws of society and to work for its welfare? The individual can, after all,
regard social development, whose fruit he himself is, as a mere fact, a result,
which he,
% --- p. 43 ---
\opage{43}%
if he has the spiritual and physical power for it, now uses in the service of
his own individual ends. It is of no use to speak of a social debt, which the
individual is to pay off by working for the continued development of society;
for the question is precisely why the individual should acknowledge this debt.
Why should the individual not regard himself as sovereign?

What individualism in its extreme forms asserts is really this, that
personality is to be an end without being a means. It does not, however,
conflict with the principle of personality that personality is a means, serves
a cause, an idea, serves society---provided only that this serving is a means to
a higher personal development for the individual himself. Man grows, as Schiller
has said, with his greater purposes. But these greater ends he can find only in
the life of society. Just as thought grows only by seeking truth, that is, by
finding as great a connection as possible between as many experiences as
possible, so the life of feeling and will grows only through the experiences
that are had and the work that is done through taking part in the life of
society and of the race. But the
% --- p. 44 ---
\opage{44}%
condition for this is that the forms and demands of society be so modified that
social work---the paying off of ``the social debt''---can serve to bring to
development powers and possibilities that would otherwise slumber unused in the
individual. Personality can make itself a means for society only when society
also makes itself a means for personality. The possibility of this is the core
of the so-called social question. In the whole of history we find great groups
of human beings who were essentially treated as means, without society
considering that it had duties toward them. All justified social reforms aim at
abolishing this dualism of end and means. Society must, if it is to continue to
exist, acknowledge the principle of personality, although this was originally
stated from individualistic standpoints.

Not all forms of individualism, however, overlook this view. Max Stirner
proclaimed (in his book ``Der Einzige und sein Eigenthum,'' 1845) a philosophy of
the self as abstract as it was absolute, according to which there was nothing,
either within us or outside us, whose demands we should acknowledge. As long as
you believe in truth,
% --- p. 45 ---
\opage{45}%
he even says, you do not believe in yourself and are only a servant. Every right
is a privilege. I acknowledge no right, and I do not care about being in the
right, if only I have power!---Here society is not merely made into a means; it
really disappears altogether. In contrast to this, an individualism or
``anarchism'' such as Kropotkin's rests on the experience of the significance
that involuntary association has for all living beings, and he has described it
in an interesting way in his book ``Mutual Aid'' (1902). Kropotkin objects only
to centralization, to the intervention of the state in the spontaneous social
formations of smaller circles. In the relations of neighborhood life orders
itself as of its own accord. What Kropotkin has no eye for is that these
involuntary associations---such as the old guilds and crafts---can lead to
stagnation in oppression and injustice, and that intervention may therefore be
needed. But he is right in this, that the state ought not, without being forced
to, intervene in the smaller circles in which life inevitably moves---for the
same reason that society ought not to intervene in the free development of the
individual
% --- p. 46 ---
\opage{46}%
personality, unless this becomes necessary out of regard for the free
development of other personalities. It is in the inner life of individual
persons that the new forces and thoughts have their origin which can carry the
life of society forward. The germ of the future sprouts there. It is thus for
its own sake that society ought to acknowledge the principle of personality. Its
real power shows itself in the strength and independence with which individual
persons can live their lives within its framework.

One often meets in modern sociologists a certain mystical conception of the
concept of society, in that they assume a ``social'' origin for ideas and
feelings. If by this it is meant only that ideas and feelings develop in
individuals through interaction with others, it is quite correct. But when
``society'' is appealed to as a cause, it is mysticism. Society as such can no
more produce law, morality and religion than it can produce poetry and science.
The ``folk songs'' were not composed by ``the people'' as a whole, but by
individual singers whose names are forgotten, because their work at once became
the property of all and because it underwent, in passing through many hands,
% --- p. 47 ---
\opage{47}%
changes that often made it into something other than it originally was. At
earlier stages of culture individual initiatives are easily forgotten. And it is
precisely such stages of culture that sociologists prefer to occupy themselves
with. At later stages the founding personalities play a greater role and are not
so easily forgotten. An appeal to ``society'' as the explanation of a phenomenon
is in fact an \textit{asylum ignorantiae}.\footnote{It is of interest in this
connection that E.~G. Geijer's defection from the ``historical school,'' which
was the romantic forerunner of modern sociology, was occasioned precisely by his
historical studies. (Saml.\ Skr.\ I, 8, p.~520). That is to say, he saw that it
was no explanation of a phenomenon to appeal to ``society'' or ``history'' en
bloc. And he saw ``that history cannot give, but can indeed modify, the
principle of action, and that this on the contrary must be each age's own
contribution.''}

This is not to deny what is justified in setting society and individual in
opposition to each other. The concept of society implies that through the life
and work of changing generations a capital is accumulated, a potential energy,
which is laid down in institutions and traditions, indeed in language itself.
Here is something that points beyond the individual,---back to
% --- p. 48 ---
\opage{48}%
the past and out toward the future,---something that belongs to no individual
whatever. These are values that are bound, and that can be set free only through
the work of individuals. A social order is valued not only according to the
capital it thus has at its disposal, but also according to the way in which it
is able to set it in motion. There can be dead values that get the appearance of
life through social institutions. Whether values are living or dead can be
decided only by whether or not they benefit the development of free
personalities. For individual personalities are both the centers in which the
values of life reveal themselves and the centers from which the work of finding
and producing values goes out.

On this whole view, society and individual stand at once as end and means for
each other.

The highest ethical thought is this, that the individual personality is to
develop itself in such a way that precisely through its independent and
distinctive development it helps others, directly or indirectly, in the best
way to develop themselves independently and distinctively. In this consists true
justice, which Westermarck rightly calls ``the
% --- p. 49 ---
\opage{49}%
flower of all social feelings.''\footnote{Justice, the flower of all moral
feelings. The origin and development of moral ideas. I p.~124.---Cf.\ my
``Etik'' III, 9; IX, 1; X, 4.} It unites harmoniously self-assertion and
devotion, the interests of the individual and of society. Through it society
lives in individuals, not only outside or above them. In it the principle of
personality comes into its full right.

% --- p. 50 ---
\opage{50}
\begin{center}\large V.\end{center}

The question can now no longer be put off whether the principle of personality,
with which we have been occupied here, can be proved to be valid. In the
immediately preceding lecture a motivation has indeed been given, but the
question may arise whether this motivation does not itself rest on certain
presuppositions. It consisted in this, that the said principle made possible the
most harmonious development of society and the most intimate interaction between
individuals.\footnote{I first gave this proof in my Etik (VIII, 6, cf.\ XIII,
6), where I also criticized Kant's artificial argument. Geijer's proof (Saml.\
skr.\ I, 5, pp.~132--137) rests chiefly on this, that the human being discovers
in every other human being a being of the same value as himself, and on this,
that the human being can develop into a personality only in interaction with
other human beings. But this does not exclude that the human being can make
these kindred beings, whose cooperation he cannot do without, into mere means
for himself!---Karl Marx (Das Kapital I p.~646) and Léon Bourgeois (in La
philosophie de la solidarité. Paris 1902, p.~38) set up the proposition as
self-evident, which a glance at history easily shows to be incorrect.} But this
line of thought is
% --- p. 51 ---
\opage{51}%
naturally accepted as valid only by those for whom such a development of the
human race stands as a good. It follows from the thought of universal human
welfare. But the question is how matters stand with this thought itself. The
answer to this question becomes decisive for the position of ethics as a
science.

Value can be measured only by value. And value points back to feeling---and
about feelings, it is said, there is no disputing. Yet when a certain value is
felt and acknowledged, one can infer from it what place other values
consistently take in relation to the given value. In every human being---and
approximately in every people and in every age---a certain fundamental value
asserts itself, an interest that lies deepest and exerts the strongest
influence. It corresponds to, or rather belongs to, what I call the real side of
personality. And it decides what else acquires value, and what value it
acquires. He for whom his own advantage is the fundamental value
% --- p. 52 ---
\opage{52}%
cannot without inconsistency make sacrifices for the benefit of other persons or
for general ends. Conversely, he in whom love of mankind determines the
fundamental value cannot without inconsistency act egoistically. Our ethical
judgments are determined by our fundamental values, and these in their turn by
our ruling feelings.

But can one now claim that all human beings in all times and under all
circumstances have the same fundamental values? That would certainly be an
unpsychological and unhistorical claim, although thinkers such as Kant and
Bentham, each in his own way, have made it. Fundamental values arise through
psychological and historical processes that are never wholly completed. A
fundamental value can never be set up as self-evident. It must grow forth, and
in each individual case it depends on how far the growth has advanced. It is of
no help to say that man grows with his greater purposes, for man must have grown
in order to be able to set himself greater purposes and then grow further again
through working for them. It serves no purpose, as Goethe has said, to tell the
caterpillar that crawls in the dust that it
% --- p. 53 ---
\opage{53}%
will one day fly from flower to flower.

The principle of personality as a general ethical principle presupposes the
development of a consciousness of humanity, a universal love of mankind, such as
first appeared in the last centuries before Christ through a peculiar fusion of
Platonic and Stoic ideas. Since then it has had a long and changing history. It
has had to struggle not only with the egoism of individuals, but also with the
egoism of families, classes, nations and confessions. In the eighteenth century
it broke through fully and set its definite demands upon civil society. But it
is always a church militant. Therefore the consequences drawn from the general
consciousness of humanity cannot be valid for all either.

Our thought can do no more than draw the consequences of certain definite
presuppositions. It is the business of experience to decide whether these
presuppositions are found everywhere and in all. This does not diminish the
significance of thought, nor that of ethical thinking. Thought can only bring to
clear consciousness what our
% --- p. 54 ---
\opage{54}%
presuppositions imply. And the significance of philosophical ethics also depends
precisely on this. It is to set out the consequences that can justly be drawn
from a certain stage in the development of human feeling. Later it can be
examined whether consequences from other stages of development coincide with
them, and what difference different motivations and different presuppositions
bring about.\footnote{This view of the scientific limitation and significance
of ethics I developed long ago in my Etik (Chap.~III). It is a joy to me to come
upon a similar line of thought in Westermarck's ``Origin and development of moral
ideas'' (I p.~17; 105; 122).}

Where ethical motivation reaches its limit, because its presuppositions are no
longer acknowledged, the question arises how these presuppositions can be
brought about. This becomes the business of moral pedagogy. It is through
upbringing that fundamental values are formed. The word upbringing is taken here
in the widest sense, so that the whole history of mankind is seen as one great
upbringing. It is the business of psychology and sociology to show how this
upbringing goes on involuntarily and unconsciously in individuals and in
generations, and
% --- p. 55 ---
\opage{55}%
what means are available when one wants to bring up human beings fully
consciously.

Schopenhauer has said that it is hard to give grounds for morality but easy to
preach morality. In the first part of his proposition he is right. But he is
wrong in holding that it should be easy to preach morality, if by this is meant
to lead human beings to such a development of their personality that by their
own strength and desire they can find what is right. Not even in upbringing may a
personal being be regarded and treated merely as a means, not even as a means
for its own future. The caterpillar should be treated as a caterpillar until it
becomes a butterfly. It is the great thought in Rousseau's pedagogy that the
child is to be treated as a child and not merely as a future adult. The point,
then, is to take the one who is to be brought up at the standpoint where he
stands, and then, by the way of self-development and self-activity, to lead him
to the level, the fundamental value, that can form the starting point for the
conclusions one wants him to draw. Here Spinoza's words hold, that there is no
way in which one can better show one's spiritual strength and capacity than by
leading others
% --- p. 56 ---
\opage{56}%
to live according to their own clear conviction.

If difficulties are thus bound up with the proof of ethical principles, there
are also difficulties in carrying them through in each individual case. Even if
the fundamental value is presupposed to be the same, the question nevertheless
arises whether, starting from the common presuppositions, one can make the same
ethical demands on all individuals. Individuals are different, both in the kind
of their faculties and in the degree of psychical energy they have at their
disposal. From this it follows that one cannot demand the same of all. Neither
what is demanded nor how much is demanded can be fixed once and for all, but must
be determined with regard to the nature and powers of the individual persons.
The same burden weighs differently on different shoulders. There are people who
have to fight a hard battle to fulfill the most elementary demands of
self-control, which others meet quite involuntarily. The ethical work can be the
same although the results turn out to be of very different value. Human beings
are like runners who begin the race from very different starting points.
% --- p. 57 ---
\opage{57}%
For it cannot be denied that in the ethical respect, as in other respects, human
beings are very differently equipped from the beginning.

It would conflict with the principle of personality to judge the individual by
an abstract rule that takes no account of his individual conditions. The task
becomes to individualize the ethical demands, so that each individual comes to
perform the work that agrees with his personal conditions, and is thereby led
onto a track that can lead further. Ethics is still far removed from such an
individualization, and it requires, after all, great psychological discernment
and great practical knowledge of human beings, as well as great patience. It is
easier to systematize and to state universally valid propositions than to find
the individual starting points in the particular cases. System and variation
stand, in ethics as in biology, in sharp opposition to each other.

In concepts such as equity and pardon, however, individualization has long been
acknowledged. Aristotle already sets up the concept of equity as a peculiar form
of justice, made
% --- p. 58 ---
\opage{58}%
necessary by the fact that there are individual nuances that prevent the
application of a general law. And that pardon is granted can be justified in law
only by the fact that legislation is not in a position to take account of all
individual circumstances. Pardon is, says Jhering, the self-correction of
justice.

It is, however, not only downward that individualization is required, but upward
as well. There are not only individuals who begin their ethical development from
a lower level than the average; there are also individuals who from the
beginning are equipped with special conditions for ethical work. And by virtue of
the principle of personality the ethical law must make greater demands on them
than on others. They must themselves demand more of themselves than is demanded
of others. They degrade themselves by contenting themselves with a minimum. It is
their ``duty'' to add ``merit.'' Schiller's Wilhelm Tell acknowledges this when
he answers his wife's complaint that his countrymen always turn to him when
something great is at stake, with the words: ``Ein Jeder wird besteuert nach
Vermögen!''

In the pedagogical respect the distinction between ``duty'' and ``merit,'' on
which
% --- p. 59 ---
\opage{59}%
Catholic theology lays so much weight, may be of importance. On the one hand it
can be hard enough to get everyone to fulfill the minimal demands one often
thinks of at the word ``duty''; on the other it may be necessary to warn against
ethical ventures, against venturing further than one's powers allow. The
difficulty lies in this, that only by trying, by venturing, do we come to know
the extent of our powers. By holding ourselves back we may cause powers and
possibilities in us that could have developed in the work for greater ends to
remain unused. And we do not come to know ourselves. Perhaps the attempt to
perform a great action leads to our coming to know our limitation. Perhaps it
leads even to a tragic outcome. But such, once and for all, are the conditions
of life. As Longfellow has said: ``Only those who brave its dangers comprehend
its mystery.'' %
% Longfellow, "The Secret of the Sea," in his own words; the Swedish renders
% them "Blott de som trotsa hafvets faror, känna hafvets hemlighet."

% --- p. 60 ---
\opage{60}
\begin{center}\large VI.\end{center}

It remains to examine the significance of the principle of personality for the
view of life and for faith, that is, its religious significance, when the word
``religious'' is taken in a wider sense. I believe that this significance is
constantly growing, and that the most essential side of the present state of the
religious problem depends upon it.

There are three elements that at any given time determine people's faith:
science, tradition and personal experience. They play a very different role in
different individuals, and even where they all assert themselves strongly, they
are united with one another in very different ways, whereby the view of life
acquires a distinct timbre. There are hardly two people in whom they appear in
the same way or are joined in the same way. It would be
% --- p. 61 ---
\opage{61}%
an interesting study to penetrate into these differences. Unfortunately such a
study is possible only to an imperfect degree with regard to contemporaries.

More and more the first two elements, science and tradition, lead to the third,
a personal experience of the course of life and the conditions of life.

a. Modern science, whose origin is to be sought in the century of the
Renaissance, has exerted a great influence on the view of life above all through
two main thoughts. The one is the idea of the immeasurability of the universe,
of the unlimited extension of space and time. The limited stage on which the
human being had until then been able to imagine the epic of existence spread out
now appeared as a vanishing point. The other is the idea of a great lawfulness
in the whole of nature, a lawfulness that is proved step by step by every change
in the world of nature or of mind being explained through its definite
connection with other changes, without causes that lie outside a possible
experience being called upon. This scientific concept of causality was applied
first in the inorganic field, later also in the
% --- p. 62 ---
\opage{62}%
organic field, as well as in the spiritual field.

The impressions this great change in the view of the world made on people's
views of life were very different. In some it awakened enthusiasm, in that
existence now more than before received the stamp of the sublime, while at the
same time the conviction became clear that it is, in spite of everything, the
same forces that are at work in human nature and in the infinite universe. In
minds such as Bruno, Böhme and Spinoza this direction in the view of life
asserts itself. In others this widening of the horizon, which turned the earthly
stage into a vanishing point, awakened horror and dizziness, and led to a more
passionate clinging to the old faith. Pascal is the great example of this
direction. There were finally those who laid no weight at all on the great
change in the picture of the world, who did not become conscious of its
significance in principle, whether in their view of life they kept to the
traditional faith or went their own ways.---Still to this day one finds these
three standpoints toward the great problem.

One need not exaggerate the significance of
% --- p. 63 ---
\opage{63}%
science in order to be sure that it will exert a rising influence on views of
life. It will not be possible to go around science. Pascal already said, after
all, that if the earth really rotates, it rotates with believers and
unbelievers alike. Science will work most through the quiet power that lies in
its method, its way of asking and answering. Less and less will people brought
up in this method be able to take mystical and anthropomorphic explanations as
real answers to the riddles of experience. It is less the special results of
science than the way of thinking it teaches people that will determine the
future of views of life. In the first section of this presentation I sought,
moreover, to bring out that it is no alien power that here comes to meet
personality. The scientific interest and the scientific capacity are closely
bound up with the nature of personality and bear its stamp. The deep longing
for wholeness that is the innermost thing in personality lies at the foundation
of science too. Science is the work of personality, which personality cannot
deny. And it is of no help to close one's eyes to the
% --- p. 64 ---
\opage{64}%
widened horizon that science opens. We must see ourselves, and the stage of all
our work and all our interests, as smaller circles within the great circle whose
center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere. Nevertheless it will
hardly ever become possible, starting from the points of view and results of
science, to construct views of life for individual personalities each by
itself. As we have seen (at the end of the second and third sections), it is
indeed the ultimate and highest task of science to understand and explain
individual totalities, and so personalities too; but here there can be question
only of approximate assumptions, and science here approaches art. For the
individual, the purely individual experiences of life will always be what is
essential in the rounding-off that he involuntarily makes when his view of life
is formed. A human being is more than an example of a general law, and even the
human life that moves in the narrowest circle can make its contribution to the
great common experience of mankind. In any case only the individual can know
what the course of life and of fate is like at the particular place within
% --- p. 65 ---
\opage{65}%
existence where his existence finds itself. ``The least defends its place in
life's wreath,'' a Danish poet has said.

b. In whatever way the religious traditions have arisen, their continued
existence and the significance they still have for so many rest on the
circumstance that the deepest experiences of life of countless generations have
been laid down in dogma and cult. The history of religion shows us how ideas
that were earlier religiously indifferent were taken into the service of
religion under the influence of great movements in the life of feeling. So it
went, for example, with the idea of the transmigration of souls in India and with
the thoughts of Greek philosophy in the dogmatics of the Christian church. But
within the religions they are regarded not merely as symbols of experiences of
life. They are fixed as dogmas, which are to contain the solution of the riddles
of life. They are torn up out of the living soil in which they grew. Yet more and
more there reveals itself in the religious consciousness of our time a rupture
between the symbolic-poetic and the literal-dogmatic conception. It appears ever
more clearly that the riddles science cannot solve are not solved by the
religious
% --- p. 66 ---
\opage{66}%
ideas either, already for the reason that science both asks and answers
otherwise than religion. The time is past when religion could satisfy all sides
of the human mind. Intellectually it has lost the battle, however it may stand
with other sides of the mind. In our day much noble strength goes to waste in the
most independent minds through the work that the rupture between religion and
science calls forth,---strength that could have been used to lay the foundation
of a conception of life based on one's own experience.

And yet it is not the relation between science and religion that is the most
important thing in the present state of the religious problem. From both sides
what can be said has been said, and continued discussion will bring nothing new.
The decisive point lies rather in the relation between religion and personality.
In contrast to the unchangeableness of religious tradition stands the free,
personal experience of life, whose richness and fullness increase in the measure
that the distinctiveness and the right of individual personalities are each
acknowledged. Every human individual is one of the centers
% --- p. 67 ---
\opage{67}%
where the value of life is discerned, and one of the starting points from which
new values can be found or produced. It is not so much before the tribunal of
science as before that of life that religion will in the future be brought. This
happens by virtue of the principle of personality. For just as little as a human
being is merely an example of a scientific concept, just so little is he to be
merely a means for the preservation of a religious tradition.\footnote{Since I
have in what precedes often referred to Erik Gustaf Geijer as one of the most
important representatives of the principle of personality, I shall quote a
remark that shows that in his religious conception too he remained true to his
principle. ``I am,'' he writes in a letter, ``neither a church Christian nor even
a Bible Christian \ldots\ I am a Christian on my own account. Thousands find
themselves in the same position as I, indeed all who cannot content themselves
with a mere faith in authority and who are compelled to build their own religion
out of their own experience and reflection. \textit{This individual element, now
the only living thing in religion} \ldots\ yet points everywhere to a common
goal.'' (Saml.\ Skr.\ I, 1, p.~XXXII~7). (The italics are mine.) Yet Geijer, who
stood under the strong influence of the romantic speculation of his time, also
gave his principle of personality a theological and metaphysical significance,
which is untenable, since every attempt in this direction leads to a play with
analogies and allegories that can neither be consistently carried through nor
confirmed by experience. Cf.\ on this my Religionsfilosofi §~32.}

% --- p. 68 ---
\opage{68}%
The religious need is a need to watch over the values of life beyond the limits
within which the human will can work for them. If the human being could himself
absolutely secure his spiritual and material possessions, religion in any form
would not arise. But as it is, hope or fear, enthusiasm or dejection, arise,
according as one feels oneself on the heights of life or in its depths. The cults
of the great religions give expression in the most exalted poetry to these
opposed moods, which the epic of life brings with it and which will always assert
themselves in everyone who lives life wholly and fully, and whose sympathy is not
blunted and narrowed.

I will not prophesy about the religion of the future. I am a philosopher, not a
prophet. But everything suggests that the more the real and lasting experiences
of life gain influence on religious life, the more the symbolic-poetic conception
of religious ideas will replace the literal-dogmatic one, and do so ever more
consciously. And with this a great step will be taken toward a free, self-won
human view of life.---

c. Science and tradition alike thus point
% --- p. 69 ---
\opage{69}%
toward personal experience of life. A thoroughgoing individualization of the view
of life is necessary. Personality is to set its stamp on its faith. Only thus
does this become a testimony to how life moves at this particular place in the
world. And as advanced psychological investigations lead us to see that
individual differences are far greater and lie far deeper than has hitherto been
assumed, the views of life of the future will probably exhibit greater oppositions
than one could get a view of so long as the common confessional dogmas covered
the mutually divergent conceptions and applications of an identically worded
confession.\footnote{Cf.\ the survey I have sought to give in my philosophy of
religion (§~36--43 \& §~94--96) of individual differences that can acquire
significance for the view of life.---We stand here at a point that has been
strongly emphasized by Danish philosophers, all the way from Holberg to Sibbern
and Kierkegaard. See on this my book ``Danska filosofer'' (1909).} It is a gleam
of light in the darkness of the time that this side of the principle of
personality is more and more acknowledged. If it is acknowledged fully, a wholly
new conception of the ultimate problems will assert itself. For the time being
% --- p. 70 ---
\opage{70}%
the old dogmas struggle with the new doubt. Through this purgatory the decisive
victory is to be won for the principle of personality.
